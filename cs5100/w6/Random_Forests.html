Random forests

Bagging, with one twist: at every split each tree may only choose from a fresh random handful of m attributes. The trees stop all copying the strongest attribute, they disagree more, and their average gets steadier.

Question type: ensemble for regression (average) and classification (vote).
Week: 6 (slides 26–27, 36).
Book: ISLP 8.2.2 (p346–347).
Techniques on this page: the random-forest algorithm, choosing \(m\), why decorrelation helps, bagging as the case \(m = p\).

The problem it solves

Bagged trees are grown from overlapping samples of the same data, so they’re correlated. If one attribute is very strong, nearly every bagged tree uses it for its first split and the trees look alike; averaging near-copies barely reduces variance. Random forests force variety.

Words you’ll hear

Word Plain meaning
\(p\) number of attributes
\(m\) number of randomly chosen candidate attributes at each split (max_features)
decorrelate make the trees’ predictions less alike

Technique 1: the algorithm

  1. For \(b = 1..B\): draw a bootstrap sample; grow a deep tree on it, but at every split pick a fresh random subset of \(m\) attributes and split on the best of those only.
  2. Average (regression) or vote (classification). OOB error works the same way.

Typical \(m\) (slide 27): \(\sqrt p\) for classification, \(p/3\) for regression. Heart data: \(p = 13\), \(m = 4\).

Technique 2: why decorrelating helps

The average of \(B\) trees, each with variance \(\sigma^2\) and pairwise correlation \(\rho\), has variance \[\rho\sigma^2 + \frac{1 - \rho}{B}\sigma^2 .\] (Not on the slides; useful.) More trees kill the second term only. Lowering \(\rho\) is the only way to shrink the first. The price: each tree is a bit worse (more bias). \(m\) is therefore a tuning parameter; choose it by OOB or validation error.

Exam sentence (q06 Q15). “A random forest differs from bagging only in that each split considers a random subset of \(m < p\) attributes. This prevents a few strong attributes from dominating every tree, so the trees are less correlated and averaging them reduces the variance more.”

Technique 3: bagging is the case m = p

If \(m = p\), every attribute is a candidate at every split: that’s ordinary tree growing on a bootstrap sample, i.e. bagging (q06 Q16).

“A random subset per tree” is wrong. The subset is redrawn at every split (slide 26). Also: smaller \(m\) isn’t always better. With many useless attributes, small \(m\) often leaves only useless candidates (the practice notebook shows this).
