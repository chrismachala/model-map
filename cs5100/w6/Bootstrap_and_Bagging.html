Bootstrap and bagging

One deep tree is clever but jumpy. Make many training sets by resampling yours with replacement, grow a deep tree on each, and average them (or let them vote). Same bias, much less variance.

Question type: ensemble meta-algorithm; regression (average) and classification (majority vote).
Week: 6 (Volodya Vovk, slides 4–18).
Book: ISLP 5.2 The bootstrap (p212–215); 8.2.1 Bagging (p343–345).
Techniques on this page: sampling with/without replacement, bootstrap samples, why averaging reduces variance, bagging for regression and classification, why more trees can’t overfit.

The problem it solves

Regression and classification trees grown deep have low bias but high variance: change the training data a little and the tree changes a lot. If we had many independent training sets we could grow a tree on each and average: averaging \(B\) independent things divides their variance by \(B\). We have one training set, so we fake the others with the bootstrap.

Words you’ll hear

Word Plain meaning
sampling without replacement take items out of the bag; each item at most once (random.sample)
sampling with replacement copy random items; repeats allowed (random.choices)
bootstrap sample (BTS) \(n\) draws with replacement from your \(n\) data points
bagging bootstrap aggregating: train on many BTSs, average or vote
meta-algorithm wraps any base learner (here, trees)
majority vote the class predicted most often by the \(B\) classifiers

Technique 1: sampling and the bootstrap

Slide 6 quiz: which are possible outputs of random.choices(range(12), k=12)? A (twelve 4s): yes. B contains 12: no (range(12) stops at 11). C: yes. D has 11 values: no.

The bootstrap idea: you can’t draw new data sets from the population, so draw them from your data, with replacement, and see how much a statistic varies across them.

Exact tiny bootstrap. Data \(\{1, 3, 8\}\), statistic = mean. All \(3^3 = 27\) resamples are equally likely; their means have standard deviation \(\sqrt{26/9} = 1.70\), the bootstrap standard error of the mean (\(= \hat\sigma/\sqrt n\) with \(\hat\sigma^2 = 26/3\)).

Technique 2: averaging reduces variance

\(n\) independent values each with variance \(\sigma^2\): their mean has variance \(\sigma^2/n\) (slide 9). The mean has the same expectation, so the same bias.

Technique 3: bagging

Regression:

  1. For \(b = 1..B\): draw a BTS \(D^{*b}\); train \(\hat f^{*b}\) on it (a deep, unpruned tree).
  2. Predict \(\hat f_{bag}(x) = \frac1B\sum_b\hat f^{*b}(x)\).

Classification: same training; predict the majority vote of the \(B\) trees.

Vote. Five trees say Yes, No, Yes, Yes, No: predict Yes.

Bias/variance: bias ≈ unchanged (so start from low-bias deep trees), variance ↓. Helps unstable learners; does almost nothing for linear regression on lots of data.

Technique 4: can more trees overfit bagging?

No (slide 18, 2025 Q3a). More trees only make the average more precise: bias unchanged, variance can only fall. The test error levels off. Pick \(B\) large enough for it to settle; \(B\) isn’t critical.

Bootstrap trees aren’t independent. They’re grown from overlapping samples, so they’re correlated and the variance doesn’t fall by the full factor \(B\). That’s what random forests fix. (Slide 9’s \(\bar Z = \frac1n\sum Z_n\) should read \(\sum_i Z_i\).)
