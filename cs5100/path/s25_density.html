Density estimation and semi-supervised learning

Where do the data crowd together? Count the points in a small window around x and divide by n times the window's size. Histograms, Parzen windows and kernel density estimates are three versions of that idea. Then: using unlabelled data to help a classifier.

Book: what to read for this stage

Density estimation and semi-supervised learning are not in ISLP. Optional: Duda, Hart and Stork 4.2–4.3 (Parzen windows), Bishop 2.5 (kernel density estimators). This stage and the study guide cover everything on slides 51–76.

1. Parametric vs non-parametric; histograms

Parametric density estimation fixes a form and fits its parameters: a Gaussian, or a mixture of Gaussians fitted with EM (2023 Q1(d)i: parametric). Non-parametric lets the data decide.

Histogram: \(f_H(x) = \frac{\#\text{ in } x\text{'s bin}}{n\,w}\). Parameters: number of bins, width, start. Drawbacks: depends on the start; jumps at edges; the curse of dimensionality: \(m\) bins per axis in \(p\) dimensions is \(m^p\) bins, so the data needed grow exponentially with \(p\).

A histogram uses 10 bins per axis in 6 dimensions. How many bins?

\(10^6 = 1{,}000{,}000\). With a few thousand points almost every bin is empty.

(2023 Q1(d)i.) Fitting a mixture of three Gaussians with the EM algorithm is…

The number of parameters doesn’t grow with the data.

2. \(f(x) \approx k/(nV)\) and Parzen windows

\(P(R) = \int_Rf \approx f(x)V\) for a small region; \(P(R) \approx k/n\); so \(f(x) \approx k/(nV)\). \(V\) large enough to hold enough points, small enough that \(f\) is flat inside.

Parzen: a box of side \(h\) centred at \(x\): in 1D count points within \(h/2\) (edges count) and divide by \(nh\); in \(p\) dimensions divide by \(nh^p\).

\(X = \{4, 5, 5, 6, 12, 14, 15, 15, 16, 17\}\), \(h = 4\): \(f(3) = 3/40 = 0.075\), \(f(10) = 1/40 = 0.025\), \(f(15) = 5/40 = 0.125\).

Data \((1, 1.7, 2, 3.4, 8)\). Parzen window estimate at \(x = 1.5\) with \(h = 2\)?

Window \([0.5, 2.5]\) holds 1, 1.7, 2: \(3/(5 \times 2) = 0.3\).

2D, \(h = 2\), at \((0, 0)\). Data \((0.5, 0.5)\), \((1, -0.9)\), \((1.5, 0)\), \((-3, 2)\). Estimate \(f(0, 0)\).

Inside \(|x_1|, |x_2| \le 1\): the first two. \(2/(4 \times 2^2) = 0.125\).

3. Smooth kernels and the bandwidth

Parzen estimates jump and weight all points in the window equally. A smooth kernel (Gaussian, triangular \(1 - |u|\), Epanechnikov) in the same formula gives a KDE: a sum of \(n\) bumps of area \(1/n\). The bandwidth \(h\) matters more than the kernel: large \(h\) over-smooths (bias), small \(h\) is spiky (variance).

You halve the bandwidth of a KDE. What happens?

Narrower bumps follow individual points.

4. Semi-supervised learning

\(l\) labelled points, \(u \gg l\) unlabelled. Two uses on the slides:

  • Re-weighting: estimate the density from the unlabelled data and weight each labelled point by how typical its region is.
  • Seeded K-means: start centre \(j\) at the mean of the seeds labelled \(j\), then ordinary K-means. Constrained K-means: the same, but seeds never change cluster.

Seeds: class A at 1 and 3, class B at 9. Unlabelled 2, 6, 7, 10. Seeded K-means (\(K = 2\)) ends with B \(=\)…

Centres 2 and 9; 6 is 4 vs 3, so B. New centres 2 and 8; nothing moves.

Stage test

(6 marks; 2023 Q1(d)ii.) Explain why histograms suffer from the curse of dimensionality.

A histogram estimates the density in each bin by the fraction of points in it divided by the bin’s volume. With \(m\) bins along each axis, a \(p\)-dimensional histogram has \(m^p\) bins, a number that grows exponentially with \(p\): 10 bins per axis in 10 dimensions gives \(10^{10}\) bins. Even a million observations leave almost every bin empty, so the estimate is zero almost everywhere and the non-empty bins rest on one or two points. To keep enough points per bin the sample size would have to grow exponentially with the dimension, so histograms are only practical in one or two dimensions.

(3 marks) Derive \(f(x) \approx \frac{k}{nV}\).

Let \(R\) be a small region around \(x\) with volume \(V\). The probability that an observation falls in \(R\) is \(P = \int_Rf(x')\,dx'\), and since \(f\) is nearly constant on a small region, \(P \approx f(x)V\). If \(k\) of the \(n\) observations fall in \(R\), the relative frequency estimates the probability: \(P \approx k/n\). Hence \(f(x) \approx k/(nV)\).

How does constrained K-means differ from seeded K-means?

Use constrained when you trust the labels.
