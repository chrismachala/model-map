Mock exam

Q1 Ensembles. (a) [4 marks] (2025 Q3a.) Can bagging overfit if the number of trees is too large? Can boosting? Justify.

Bagging cannot overfit by adding trees: they are grown independently and averaged, so more trees leave the bias unchanged and only reduce the variance of the average; the test error levels off. Boosting can: each new tree fits the remaining training residuals, so eventually it fits the noise and the test error rises (slowly for small \(\eta\)); \(B\) must be chosen by validation or cross-validation.

Q1 (c) [4 marks] Write pseudocode for computing the OOB error of a bagged classifier, and say why it’s preferable to cross-validation.

For \(i = 1, \dots, n\): let \(S_i\) be the set of trees whose bootstrap sample does not contain \((x_i, y_i)\); if \(S_i\) is empty skip \(i\); otherwise \(\hat y_i^{OOB}\) = majority vote of the trees in \(S_i\) at \(x_i\). OOB error \(= \frac{1}{\#\text{used}}\sum_i\mathbb 1(\hat y_i^{OOB} \ne y_i)\). Each vote comes from trees that never saw the point, so it estimates test error, and it reuses the trees already grown instead of refitting the ensemble \(K\) times as cross-validation would.

Q2 Clustering. (a) [6 marks] (2023 Q5a.) True or false, with a short justification each: (i) K-means is deterministic. (ii) With \(K = 10\), K-means outputs 10 clusters. (iii) With single linkage and 5 clusters, the furthest pair of points can be in the same cluster.

  1. False. K-means starts from a random assignment and converges to a local minimum that depends on it; different runs can give different clusterings, which is why it is restarted several times. (ii) False. During reassignment a cluster can lose all its points (e.g. \(-3, -2, 2, 3\) with \(K = 3\) starting from \(\{-3\}, \{3\}, \{-2, 2\}\) ends with two clusters). (iii) True. Single linkage merges clusters by their closest points, so a long chain of closely spaced points becomes one cluster even though its ends are the furthest pair.

Q3 Density estimation. (a) [4 marks] Explain the role of the bandwidth \(h\) in kernel density estimation, and why the Parzen window is often replaced by a smooth kernel.

\(f(x) = \frac{1}{nh^p}\sum_iK\big(\frac{x - x_i}{h}\big)\) puts a bump of width about \(h\) on each observation. A large \(h\) gives wide bumps that blur real structure, such as merging two peaks (high bias, low variance); a small \(h\) gives a spike at each observation that changes from sample to sample (low bias, high variance). \(h\) is chosen to balance the two, by a rule of thumb or cross-validation, and matters much more than the kernel’s shape. The Parzen window is replaced by a smooth kernel because its estimate jumps whenever a point enters or leaves the window, and it counts a point at the window’s edge as much as one at its centre.
