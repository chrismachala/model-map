Dimensionality reduction and PCA

Too many attributes: the space empties out exponentially and you can't see it. Reduce p to M by selecting or extracting features. PCA extracts them: the direction of largest variance, then the largest at right angles, and so on. By hand on small symmetric data, like slides 27 and 28.

Book: what to read for this stage

ISLP 12.2 intro, 12.2.1 and 12.2.2 (p504–510). The slides cite “James et al. Section 10.2”: that’s the old edition; in ISLP it’s 12.2. Skip 12.3 (matrix completion). The curse of dimensionality is in ISLP 3.5 (p115) and 6.4.4 if you want more. The study guide W8_Study_Guide sections 1–4 cover this stage in full.

1. The curse of dimensionality

Cut each axis into \(m\) pieces: \(m^p\) cells (\(3, 9, 27\) on slide 8). Estimating anything needs data in each region, so the data needed grow exponentially with \(p\). For a fixed \(n\), beyond some number of features a classifier gets worse. Escape routes: prior knowledge, smoother rules, fewer dimensions (computation, generalisation, and mainly visualisation).

Points are uniform in \([0, 1]^3\). What fraction, on average, falls in the corner cube \([0, 0.1]^3\)?

\(0.1^3 = 0.001\). In 10 dimensions it would be \(10^{-10}\): neighbourhoods are empty.

2. Selection vs extraction

Selection keeps a subset of the attributes (cheap to measure, interpretable, Occam’s razor; a projection onto an axis-aligned subspace). Extraction builds new ones from combinations, usually linear \(z = Ax\). Linear extraction for representation (no labels): PCA. For classification (labels): LDA.

Which is a reason to prefer feature selection?

The second and third describe extraction (PCA keeps at least as much variance as any \(M\) attributes); the fourth is LDA.

3. Covariance, loadings, scores

Centre the columns (and scale if units differ). \(\Sigma = \frac1nX^TX\) (scatter matrix \(S = n\Sigma = \sum(x_i - \mu)(x_i - \mu)^T\)). The first PC \(Z_1 = \varphi_1^TX\) maximises the variance \(\varphi^T\Sigma\varphi\) over unit vectors \(\varphi\). \(\varphi_1\) = loading vector, \(z_{i1} = \varphi_1^Tx_i\) = scores = projections onto the direction \(\varphi_1\).

\((3, 1), (1, 3), (-1, -3), (-3, -1)\): \(\Sigma = \begin{pmatrix}5 & 3\\3 & 5\end{pmatrix}\). Along \((\cos\theta, \sin\theta)\) the variance is \(5 + 3\sin2\theta\): max \(8\) at \(45°\). So \(\varphi_1 = \frac1{\sqrt2}(1, 1)\), \(\lambda_1 = 8\); \(\varphi_2 = \frac1{\sqrt2}(1, -1)\), \(\lambda_2 = 2\); PVE of PC1 \(80\%\).

(2023 Q1(c)i.) Compute \((1, 2, -3) \cdot (5, -4, -1)\). (So are they orthogonal?)

\(5 - 8 + 3 = 0\): yes, orthogonal.

Why must the loadings satisfy \(\sum_j\varphi_{j1}^2 = 1\)?

Slide 22. Mean zero comes from centring; uncorrelated from orthogonality.

4. Later PCs and eigenvectors

\(Z_2\): the largest variance among unit combinations uncorrelated with \(Z_1\), i.e. \(\varphi_2 \perp \varphi_1\); and so on to \(Z_p\). Loading vectors = eigenvectors of \(\Sigma\), variances = eigenvalues; \(\sum\lambda_m\) = total variance. Signs are arbitrary.

Slide 28. \((-2, 0), (2, 0), (0, 1), (0, -1)\): no covariance, so the axes: \(\lambda_1 = \frac{4 + 4}{4} = 2\) along \((1, 0)\), \(\lambda_2 = \frac{1 + 1}4 = 0.5\) along \((0, 1)\).

Slide 27. Observations \((-1, -1)\) and \((1, 1)\). The second PC’s variance is…

PC1 \(\frac1{\sqrt2}(1, 1)\) has scores \(\mp\sqrt2\), variance 2; everything lies on that line.

Stage test

(7 marks; 2023 Q1(c)ii.) You are asked to perform PCA on a set of data points in \(\mathbb{R}^{2023}\). Describe how the scatter matrix is computed, discussing all pre-processing steps.

Put the \(n\) observations as the rows of an \(n \times 2023\) data matrix. First centre the data: compute the mean of each attribute, \(\mu_j = \frac1n\sum_ix_{ij}\), and subtract the mean vector \(\mu\) from every observation, so that every column has mean zero (PCA describes variation around the mean). If the attributes are measured in different units or have very different spreads, also divide each column by its standard deviation, so that every attribute has variance one; otherwise the attributes with the largest numbers dominate the components. (Missing values and gross outliers should be dealt with first.) The scatter matrix is then \(S = \sum_{i=1}^n(x_i - \mu)(x_i - \mu)^T\), which for the centred (and scaled) matrix is \(X^TX\): a symmetric \(2023 \times 2023\) matrix whose \((j, k)\) entry is \(\sum_ix_{ij}x_{ik}\). Dividing by \(n\) gives the covariance matrix (the correlation matrix if the data were scaled); the constant doesn’t change the eigenvectors. The eigenvectors of \(S\), ordered by decreasing eigenvalue, are the principal component loading vectors, and the eigenvalues are proportional to the variances of the components.

(6 marks; 2023 Q1(d)ii.) Describe the curse of dimensionality in the context of density estimation with a histogram.

A histogram estimates the density in a bin by the fraction of observations in it divided by the bin’s volume. With \(m\) bins along each axis a \(p\)-dimensional histogram has \(m^p\) bins, which grows exponentially with \(p\): 10 bins per axis in 10 dimensions is \(10^{10}\) bins. Even a million observations leave almost every bin empty, so the estimate is zero almost everywhere and the non-empty bins rest on one or two points. To keep enough points per bin the sample size would have to grow exponentially with the dimension, so histograms are only practical in one or two dimensions.

That’s what LDA is for (slide 15).
