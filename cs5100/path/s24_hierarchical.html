Hierarchical clustering

Start with every point alone and keep merging the two closest clusters. "Closest" needs a rule for clusters: closest pair, furthest pair, average pair or centroids. Each rule has its own failure, and the 2023 paper asked about both.

Book: what to read for this stage

ISLP 12.4.2 Hierarchical clustering (p525–532, Algorithm 12.3, Table 12.3, Fig 12.12): read. The parts on correlation-based distance and scaling: skim. 12.4.3 Practical issues: skim.

1. Agglomerative clustering and the dendrogram

  1. Each point is a cluster. 2. Repeatedly fuse the two clusters with the smallest linkage, drawing the fuse at that height, until one cluster is left.

The dendrogram shows merges as joins at a height equal to the dissimilarity. Read it vertically only: side-by-side leaves aren’t necessarily close. Cut it with a horizontal line to get flat clusters.

2. The four linkages

linkage distance between clusters
single closest pair
complete furthest pair
average mean over all \(n_jn_k\) pairs
centroid distance between the means

Method: write the full table of cross distances, then read off min, max and mean; compute the centroids separately.

2023 Q5(b). \(\{(3, 4), (5, 2)\}\) vs \(\{(-1, -1), (2, -4), (2, -1)\}\): complete \(\sqrt{65} = 8.06\), single \(\sqrt{18} = 4.24\), average 6.20, centroid \(\sqrt{34} = 5.83\).

Clusters \(\{(0, 0), (0, 2)\}\) and \(\{(3, 0), (5, 0)\}\). Complete linkage?

Cross distances 3, 5, \(\sqrt{13} = 3.61\), \(\sqrt{29} = 5.39\). Largest \(\sqrt{29} = 5.385\).

Same clusters. Centroid linkage?

Centroids \((0, 1)\) and \((4, 0)\): \(\sqrt{16 + 1} = \sqrt{17} = 4.123\).

3. AHC by hand in 1D

Slide 44, single linkage. \(\{1, 3, 4, 13, 28, 29\}\), ties to the larger mean: \(\{28, 29\}\) at 1, \(\{3, 4\}\) at 1, \(\{1, 3, 4\}\) at 2, \(+13\) at 9, all at 15.

Complete linkage on \(\{0, 2, 5, 11, 12\}\). Merges: \(\{11, 12\}\) at 1, \(\{0, 2\}\) at 2. At what height does 5 join?

5 to \(\{0, 2\}\): \(\max(5, 3) = 5\). 5 to \(\{11, 12\}\): 7. So it joins \(\{0, 2\}\) at 5. (Last merge at \(12 - 0 = 12\).)

4. Chaining and inversions

Single linkage chains. Two clusters merge once any two of their points are close, so a long line of closely spaced points merges into one cluster whose ends are far apart. With 5 clusters the two furthest points can share one (2023 Q5(a)iii: true).

Centroid linkage inverts. \(A = (0, 0)\), \(B = (2, 0)\), \(C = (1, 1.8)\): \(A, B\) merge at 2, their centroid \((1, 0)\) is 1.8 from \(C\), so the next merge is lower.

Divisive clustering goes top-down (split the worst cluster, e.g. with 2-means); it’s costlier, so agglomerative is more popular.

Stage test

(4 marks; 2023 Q5(a)iii.) True or false: with single linkage and 5 output clusters, the two furthest-apart observations can belong to the same cluster. Justify.

True. Single linkage measures the distance between clusters by their closest pair, so a chain of points with small gaps is merged step by step into one long cluster, however far apart its ends are. Example: 101 points at \(-50, -49, \dots, 50\) on a line plus four tight groups placed more than 1 unit from the line and from each other; the line merges first (all gaps 1), and cutting at 5 clusters leaves the line as one cluster containing \((-50, 0)\) and \((50, 0)\), the furthest pair.

Which linkage can produce an inversion in the dendrogram?

A merged cluster’s centroid can be closer to a third cluster than the two parts were to each other.

Tick every true statement comparing K-means and agglomerative clustering.

AHC needs all \(n(n-1)/2\) distances, so it scales worse.
