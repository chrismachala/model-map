PCA in practice: how many PCs, scaling, biplots

p attributes give p principal components; the reduction comes from keeping only the first M. Choose M with the proportion of variance explained, a scree plot or the eigenvalue-one rule, scale the attributes first when their units differ, and read the loadings to say what each component means.

Question type: using PCA: choosing \(M\), interpreting loadings, pre-processing.
Week: 8 (slides 29–47).
Book: ISLP 12.2.3 (PVE, p510–512) and 12.2.4 (scaling, uniqueness, how many PCs, p512–515); lab 12.5.1.
Past paper: not yet examined in this shape; 2023 Q1(c)ii’s “all pre-processing steps” includes scaling.
Techniques on this page: PVE and cumulative PVE, scree plots, the eigenvalue-one criterion, scaling, reading a biplot, eigenfaces and reconstruction.

The problem it solves

PCA always returns \(p\) components, and using all of them is just a rotation. You need a rule for how many to keep, a check that one attribute isn’t dominating because of its units, and a way to say what the kept components mean. There’s no accepted objective rule for \(M\) (slide 33), so you need to know all four and argue for one.

Words you’ll hear

Word Plain meaning
PVE proportion of variance explained: \(\lambda_m / \sum_j\lambda_j\)
cumulative PVE the PVE of the first \(M\) together
scree plot PVE (or \(\lambda\)) against \(m\); keep what’s left of the elbow
eigenvalue-one (Kaiser) keep PCs with \(\lambda > 1\), for standardised data
biplot scores as points and loadings as arrows on one plot
eigenfaces loading vectors of face images, viewed as images

Technique 1: choosing M (slides 31–33, 42–43)

  1. PVE threshold: smallest \(M\) with \(\frac{\sum_{m \le M}\lambda_m}{\sum_m\lambda_m} > \theta\).
  2. Scree test: keep the PCs to the left of the elbow (scree = the rubble at the foot of a cliff).
  3. Eigenvalue-one: keep \(\lambda_m > 1\) (standardised attributes only: each attribute has variance 1).
  4. Interpretability: keep what you can explain.

USArrests, standardised (\(\frac1n\)): \(\lambda = 2.480, 0.990, 0.357, 0.173\) (sum 4). PVE \(62.0\%, 24.7\%, 8.9\%, 4.3\%\); cumulative \(62.0, 86.8, 95.7, 100\). \(\theta = 0.8\): \(M = 2\); \(\theta = 0.9\): \(M = 3\); scree: \(M = 2\); eigenvalue-one: \(M = 1\), but sklearn’s \(\frac1{n-1}\) variances give \(\lambda_2 = 1.010\), so \(M = 2\). Borderline: state your convention.

Technique 2: scaling (slide 41)

Unscaled USArrests: Assault’s variance (≈ 6945) dwarfs the rest, so PC1 is Assault (loading 0.995) and “explains” 96.6%. Standardise (StandardScaler) when the attributes are in different units; you can skip it when they share units (pixels, gene expression).

Technique 3: reading loadings and biplots (slides 38–40)

PC1 PC2
Murder 0.54 −0.42
Assault 0.58 −0.19
UrbanPop 0.28 0.87
Rape 0.54 0.17

Columns = loading vectors. PC1 ≈ overall crime (three crimes about equal); PC2 ≈ urbanisation. Biplot arrows are the rows; the crime arrows point together because the crimes are correlated. The lab’s plot is slide 39 mirrored: signs are arbitrary.

A state with standardised values \((1.2, 0.8, -0.5, 0.3)\): \(z_1 = 0.54(1.2) + 0.58(0.8) + 0.28(-0.5) + 0.54(0.3) = 1.134\); \(z_2 = -0.504 - 0.152 - 0.435 + 0.051 = -1.04\).

Technique 4: reconstruction, compression, eigenfaces (slides 44–47)

\(x_i \approx \bar x + \sum_{m=1}^Mz_{im}\varphi_m\). The average squared reconstruction error equals the sum of the dropped eigenvalues. With faces, the \(\varphi_m\) reshaped as images are eigenfaces; a few dozen scores give a fair likeness (intrinsic dimension ≈ 30), so they’re used for compression and recognition.

Technique 5: the main limitation (slide 29)

PCA ignores labels: maximum-variance directions need not separate the classes. For that, use LDA (slide 15).

Lab Exercise 3: explained_variance_ratio_ = explained_variance_ / explained_variance_.sum() holds only because PCA() kept all components. With n_components=2, divide by the total variance of the data. Lab text: the scores come from transform(), not fit().
