Principal component analysis (PCA)

Find the direction along which the data are most spread out, then the most spread-out direction at right angles to it, and so on. Each direction is a weighted sum of the attributes; keep the first few and you've shrunk the data while keeping most of the variance.

Question type: unsupervised: dimensionality reduction by linear feature extraction.
Week: 8 (slides 17–28).
Book: ISLP 12.2 intro, 12.2.1 and 12.2.2 (p504–510). The slides’ “James et al. Section 10.2” is the old edition’s numbering.
Past paper: 2023 Q1(c) (orthogonal vectors 3 marks; computing the scatter matrix with all pre-processing, 7 marks).
Techniques on this page: centring and the covariance/scatter matrix, loadings and scores, the variance of a projection \(\varphi^T\Sigma\varphi\), PCA by hand, eigenvectors.

The problem it solves

\(p\) attributes are too many to look at and often say the same thing twice (crime rates that rise together). PCA finds a new set of variables, the principal components, that are uncorrelated, ordered by variance, and fewer than \(p\) while keeping nearly all the variance. It’s the oldest multivariate method (Pearson 1901, Hotelling 1930s) and usually a step inside something bigger: plotting, regression, clustering, compression.

Words you’ll hear

Word Plain meaning
principal component \(Z_m\) a new variable: a weighted sum of the attributes
loading vector \(\varphi_m\) the weights; a unit vector, i.e. a direction
loadings \(\varphi_{jm}\) the individual weights
scores \(z_{im}\) each observation’s value on PC \(m\): its projection \(\varphi_m^Tx_i\)
covariance matrix \(\Sigma\) \(p \times p\) table of variances (diagonal) and covariances
scatter matrix \(S\) \(\sum_i(x_i - \mu)(x_i - \mu)^T = n\Sigma\)
eigenvector / eigenvalue a direction \(\Sigma\) only stretches, and by how much: the PCs and their variances

Technique 1: centre, then the covariance (scatter) matrix

Put the observations as rows of the \(n \times p\) data matrix \(X\). Centre each column (subtract its mean); scale each to standard deviation 1 if the units differ. Then

\[\Sigma = \frac1nX^TX, \qquad S = \sum_{i=1}^n(x_i - \mu)(x_i - \mu)^T = n\Sigma.\]

Diagonal = variances, off-diagonal = covariances. The constant in front doesn’t change the PCs’ directions.

\((3, 1), (1, 3), (-1, -3), (-3, -1)\): mean 0; \(\Sigma_{11} = \Sigma_{22} = \frac{9 + 1 + 1 + 9}{4} = 5\), \(\Sigma_{12} = \frac{3 \cdot 4}{4} = 3\). So \(\Sigma = \begin{pmatrix}5 & 3\\3 & 5\end{pmatrix}\), \(S = 4\Sigma\).

Technique 2: the first PC maximises the projected variance (slides 22–23)

\(Z_1 = \varphi_{11}X_1 + \dots + \varphi_{p1}X_p\) with \(\sum_j\varphi_{j1}^2 = 1\) (otherwise the variance could be made as large as you like) and largest variance. The scores \(z_{i1} = \varphi_1^Tx_i\) are the projections of the points onto the direction \(\varphi_1\). For centred data and a unit \(\varphi\):

\[\text{variance of the scores} = \frac1n\sum_i(\varphi^Tx_i)^2 = \varphi^T\Sigma\varphi.\]

Same data: along \((1, 0)\) the variance is \(5\); along \(\frac1{\sqrt2}(1, 1)\) it’s \(\frac12(5 + 3 + 3 + 5) = 8\); in general \(5 + 3\sin2\theta\). So \(\varphi_1 = \frac1{\sqrt2}(1, 1)\), \(\lambda_1 = 8\); scores \(\pm2\sqrt2\).

Technique 3: later PCs, orthogonality and eigenvectors (slides 24–26)

\(Z_2\): largest variance among unit combinations uncorrelated with \(Z_1\), which is the same as \(\varphi_2 \perp \varphi_1\). And so on up to \(Z_p\). The loading vectors are the eigenvectors of \(\Sigma\) and the variances \(\lambda_1 \ge \lambda_2 \ge \dots\) its eigenvalues (general definitions optional, slide 26). \(\sum_m\lambda_m\) = total variance = sum of the diagonal of \(\Sigma\) (\(= p\) for standardised data). Loading vectors are unique only up to sign.

Optional 2×2 shortcut: for \(\begin{pmatrix}a & b\\b & c\end{pmatrix}\), \(\lambda = \frac{a + c}2 \pm \sqrt{(\frac{a - c}2)^2 + b^2}\), \(\varphi_1 \propto (b, \lambda_1 - a)\).

Technique 4: the slides’ toy examples by hand (slides 27–28)

Slide 27. \((-1, -1), (1, 1)\). PC1 \(\frac1{\sqrt2}(1, 1)\), scores \(\mp\sqrt2\), variance \(\frac12(2 + 2) = \mathbf 2\). PC2 \(\frac1{\sqrt2}(1, -1)\), scores \(0, 0\), variance \(\mathbf 0\).

Slide 28. \((-2, 0), (2, 0), (0, 1), (0, -1)\). No covariance, so the axes. PC1 \((1, 0)\): scores \(-2, 2, 0, 0\), \(\lambda_1 = \frac{8}{4} = \mathbf 2\). PC2 \((0, 1)\): \(\lambda_2 = \frac{2}{4} = \mathbf{0.5}\). (The diagonal only gets \(1.25\).)

\(\frac1n\) vs \(\frac1{n-1}\). The slides and ISLP divide by \(n\); scikit-learn’s explained_variance_ divides by \(n - 1\). Same directions, same PVE, variances bigger by \(\frac{n}{n-1}\). Slide 58’s “Section 10.2” is ISLP 12.2.

Technique 5: the 2023 exam question

“Describe how the scatter matrix of a data set in \(\mathbb{R}^{2023}\) is computed, with all pre-processing steps” (7 marks): data as rows of \(X\); centre (subtract the mean vector); scale each attribute to unit standard deviation if units/spreads differ; (handle missing values/outliers); \(S = \sum_i(x_i - \mu)(x_i - \mu)^T = X^TX\), a symmetric \(2023 \times 2023\) matrix; its eigenvectors ordered by eigenvalue are the loading vectors. And part (i): vectors are orthogonal iff their dot product is 0: \((1, 2, -3) \cdot (5, -4, -1) = 5 - 8 + 3 = 0\).
