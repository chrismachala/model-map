Dimensionality reduction

Too many attributes to look at, too few observations to fill the space. Shrink p attributes down to M: keep a few of the old ones (feature selection) or build a few new ones from all of them (feature extraction). This page is the "why"; PCA, MDS and t-SNE are the "how".

Question type: unsupervised: turn \(n \times p\) data into \(n \times M\) data, \(M < p\).
Week: 8 (slides 4–16).
Book: ISLP 12.1 (p503–504); the curse of dimensionality in ISLP 3.5 (p115) and 6.4.4 (p266) is optional. Duda et al. 3.7 and Bishop 1.4 are the slides’ sources.
Past paper: 2023 Q1(d)ii (curse of dimensionality for histograms, 6 marks).
Techniques on this page: the curse of dimensionality, selection vs extraction, linear feature extraction, PCA vs LDA.

The problem it solves

You can only see two or three dimensions, and with \(p = 10\) attributes there are already 45 pairwise scatter plots, none showing the whole picture. Worse, every model that relies on “nearby” data (KNN, histograms, kernel density estimates) needs exponentially more data as \(p\) grows. Reducing the dimension helps with computation (less to store), statistics (less noise, better generalisation) and, above all, visualisation (slide 6).

Words you’ll hear

Word Plain meaning
EDA exploratory data analysis: look at the data before modelling
dimension, \(p\) number of attributes (features) per observation
curse of dimensionality the space, and the data needed to fill it, grows exponentially with \(p\)
feature selection keep a subset of the original attributes
feature extraction build new attributes as combinations of the old ones
linear projection new features \(z = Ax\): weighted sums of the old ones
intrinsic dimension how many numbers really describe the data (about 30 for faces)

Technique 1: the curse of dimensionality (slides 7–9)

Cut each axis into \(m\) pieces: \(m^p\) cells (slide 8: \(3, 9, 27\) for \(D = 1, 2, 3\)). Estimating a function needs data in every region, so the data needed grow exponentially with \(p\). For a fixed sample size there’s a number of features beyond which a classifier gets worse; the information lost by dropping features is usually more than paid back by a more accurate rule in fewer dimensions (slide 7).

How empty is high-dimensional space? 1,000 points uniform in \([0, 1]^p\); expected number in the corner cube \([0, 0.1]^p\): \(100\) (\(p = 1\)), \(10\) (\(p = 2\)), \(1\) (\(p = 3\)), \(10^{-7}\) (\(p = 10\)).

Ways out (slide 9): prior knowledge, smoother prediction rules, fewer dimensions.

Technique 2: selection vs extraction (slides 10–12)

Feature selection Feature extraction
What keep a subset of attributes new attributes from combinations
Why features cost money to measure; kept ones are interpretable; Occam’s razor keeps information from all attributes
Geometry projection onto an axis-aligned subspace projection onto any subspace (if linear)
Test time fast: just read the kept columns compute the combinations

Technique 3: linear feature extraction, PCA vs LDA (slides 13–16)

Look for \(f: \mathbb{R}^p \to \mathbb{R}^M\) that keeps most of the information. The optimal \(f\) is usually non-linear and there’s no systematic way to find it, so restrict to linear \(z = Ax\). Two criteria:

  • Signal representation (no labels): represent the data accurately. PCA.
  • Classification accuracy (uses labels): keep what separates the classes. LDA (Fisher’s view of the week 3 classifier: the direction along which the class means are far apart relative to the spread within classes).

Other representation methods: factor analysis (hidden latent factors) and MDS (preserve distances).

Slide 15 says LDA is “discussed in other modules”, but you met it in week 3 as a classifier. It’s the same model seen as a projection.
