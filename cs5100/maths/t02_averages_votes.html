Averages, indicators and votes

The humblest tool in the course is also the most used: average the numbers, or count the labels and take the most common one. Do that over "the nearby points" and you have KNN, a tree leaf, a K-means centre and a density estimate. Do it over many models and you have bagging.

Book and slides

No single section: this idea is spread through ISLP 2.2.2 to 2.2.3 (error rate, KNN), 3.5 (KNN regression), 8.1 (tree leaves), 8.2.1 (bagging) and 12.4.1 (K-means centres). Week 7 slides, “Non-parametric density estimation”, derive \(f(x) \approx k/(nV)\). Nothing to skip here; it’s all used.

1. The mean and the indicator

The mean of \(y_1, \dots, y_n\) is \(\bar y = \frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n y_i\). For vectors, average each coordinate: the result is the centroid.

The indicator \(\mathbb 1(\text{statement})\) is 1 when the statement is true and 0 when false. Its job: turning “how many?” questions into sums.

The key trick: a proportion is a mean of indicators.

\[\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n\mathbb 1(y_i = k) = \text{fraction of points with label } k.\]

That one line is the error rate (with \(\mathbb 1(\hat y_i \ne y_i)\), week 1), KNN’s probability estimate (week 1), a tree node’s class proportion \(\hat p_{mk}\) (week 4), and the histogram density (week 7).

Worked example. Labels \((A, B, A, A, C)\). \(\hat p_A = \frac15(1 + 0 + 1 + 1 + 0) = 0.6\). Predictions \((A, A, A, B, C)\) against those labels: mistakes at points 2 and 4, so the error rate is \(\frac15(0 + 1 + 0 + 1 + 0) = 0.4\).

True labels \((1, 0, 0, 1, 1, 0, 1, 0)\), predictions \((1, 0, 1, 1, 0, 0, 1, 0)\). Compute the error rate \(\frac1n\sum\mathbb 1(\hat y_i \ne y_i)\).

Mismatches at positions 3 and 5: \(2/8 = 0.25\).

2. Vote: take the most common label

To predict a class from a group of labels, take the majority vote: \(\hat y = \arg\max_k\hat p_k\), the class with the biggest proportion. “\(\arg\max\)” means “the \(k\) that makes this biggest”, not the biggest value itself. The same \(\arg\max\) appears in the Bayes classifier (pick the most probable class) and in softmax models (pick the largest probability).

3. Do it locally: the “neighbourhood average”

Most non-parametric methods in the course are this one idea: pick a group of training points near \(x\), then average (numbers) or vote (labels). Only the rule for “near” changes:

Method Who is “near” \(x\) Then
KNN classifier (w1) the \(K\) closest points vote; \(\tilde P(Y{=}k\mid x) = \frac1K\sum\mathbb 1(y_i = k)\)
KNN regression (w2) the \(K\) closest points average: \(\hat y = \frac1K\sum y_i\)
Regression tree (w4) points in the same box (leaf) average the leaf’s \(y\)
Classification tree (w4) points in the same box vote
Histogram / \(k/(nV)\) (w7) points in a bin or region of volume \(V\) count, divide by \(nV\)
K-means update (w7) points assigned to centre \(j\) average them: the new centre

And one more, where the “group” is a set of models rather than points: bagging (w6) averages \(B\) trees’ predictions (regression) or takes their majority vote (classification).

Worked example: one idea, three answers. Training data \(x = (1, 2, 4, 7, 8)\), \(y = (3, 5, 4, 10, 12)\). Predict at \(x_0 = 2.5\).

  • KNN regression, \(K = 3\): nearest are \(x = 2, 1, 4\) (distances 0.5, 1.5, 1.5), so \(\hat y = (5 + 3 + 4)/3 = 4\).
  • A tree with one split at \(x < 5.5\): \(x_0\) is in the left box \(\{1, 2, 4\}\), so \(\hat y = 4\) (the same three points this time).
  • Density with region \([1, 4]\) (width \(V = 3\)): \(k = 3\) of \(n = 5\) points, \(f(2.5) \approx 3/(5 \times 3) = 0.2\).

4. The centroid

For vectors, average coordinate by coordinate: \(\mu = \frac{1}{n}\sum_i x_i\). The centroid is what K-means moves each centre to and what the centroid linkage measures between. Why the mean and not, say, the median? Because the mean is the point that minimises the sum of squared distances, which Toolkit 3 proves.

Stage test

The 5 nearest neighbours of \(x_0\) have labels red, blue, red, red, blue. What is KNN’s estimate \(\tilde P(Y = \text{red}\mid x_0)\) with \(K = 5\)?

\(\frac15\sum\mathbb 1(y_i = \text{red}) = 3/5 = 0.6\). (The week 1 slides’ formula (33) forgets the \(\frac1K\); without it you’d get a count, not a probability.)

A regression tree leaf holds training responses \(4, 6, 8, 10\). Bagging averages three trees whose predictions at \(x_0\) are \(6\), \(7\) and \(8\). What does the bagged model predict at \(x_0\)?

Average of the three tree predictions: \((6 + 7 + 8)/3 = 7\). (The leaf values matter only for one tree’s prediction; that tree would say \(7\).)

Which of these are “average or vote over a neighbourhood” methods? Tick all that apply.

Logistic regression uses a global formula \(\sigma(\beta\cdot\tilde x)\) fitted to all points; it doesn’t look at a local group at prediction time.
