The Gaussian bell curve

One density, three jobs: it describes each class in LDA and naive Bayes, it is the noise assumption that secretly justifies least squares, and it is the smooth "bump" in kernel density estimation. The trick that makes it so useful: its log is a squared distance.

Book and slides

ISLP 4.4.1 (LDA with one input, eq. 4.16 to 4.18: the Gaussian density and the discriminant). Skim 4.4.2 (the multivariate version with a covariance matrix \(\mathbf{\Sigma}\)): know it exists, don’t learn the algebra. Week 7 slides, “Smooth kernels”, for the Gaussian kernel \(K(x) = (2\pi)^{-p/2}e^{-\frac12x^Tx}\). The week 7 slides say parametric (Gaussian) density estimation is “not in this module”, but the Gaussian kernel is.

1. The formula

A Gaussian (normal) distribution with mean \(\mu\) and variance \(\sigma^2\) has density

\[\boxed{f(x) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma^2}}\exp\Big(-\frac{(x - \mu)^2}{2\sigma^2}\Big)}\]

  • \(\mu\) is the centre (the peak). \(\sigma\) is the spread: about 68% of the mass is within \(\mu \pm \sigma\), 95% within \(\mu \pm 2\sigma\).
  • The front factor \(\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma^2}}\) just makes the area 1. The shape is all in the exponent.
  • A density can be bigger than 1 (a narrow Gaussian with \(\sigma = 0.1\) peaks at about 4). It’s area, not height, that’s a probability.

Worked example. \(\mu = 2\), \(\sigma = 1\), \(x = 3\). Exponent term \((3 - 2)^2/2 = 0.5\). \(f(3) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}e^{-0.5} = 0.3989 \times 0.6065 = 0.242\).

2. Its log is a squared distance

\[\log f(x) = \underbrace{-\tfrac12\log(2\pi\sigma^2)}_{\text{constant}} - \frac{(x - \mu)^2}{2\sigma^2}.\]

So “make \(f(x)\) big” means “make \((x - \mu)^2\) small”. That is the bridge between probability and squared error (Toolkit 3), and it pays off three times:

  1. Least squares is maximum likelihood. If \(y_i = f(x_i) + \epsilon_i\) with Gaussian noise, the log-likelihood of the data is a constant minus \(\frac{1}{2\sigma^2}\sum(y_i - f(x_i))^2\). Maximising it is minimising RSS (w2, revisited in w3).
  2. The best estimate of \(\mu\) is the mean. Maximising \(\prod f(x_i)\) over \(\mu\) means minimising \(\sum(x_i - \mu)^2\), so \(\hat\mu = \bar x\) (Toolkit 3 again). That’s why LDA estimates each class centre as the class average.
  3. LDA’s boundary is linear (next step).

3. Gaussian + Bayes’ rule = LDA

LDA gives every class a Gaussian with its own mean \(\mu_k\) but the same variance \(\sigma^2\). Take logs of \(\pi_kf_k(x)\) and drop anything that doesn’t depend on \(k\):

\[\log\big(\pi_kf_k(x)\big) = \text{const} - \frac{x^2}{2\sigma^2} + \frac{x\mu_k}{\sigma^2} - \frac{\mu_k^2}{2\sigma^2} + \log\pi_k.\]

The \(x^2\) term is the same for every class, so it can’t change which class wins. What’s left is the discriminant

\[\delta_k(x) = \frac{x\,\mu_k}{\sigma^2} - \frac{\mu_k^2}{2\sigma^2} + \log\pi_k,\]

which is linear in \(x\): a dot product plus a constant (Toolkit 1). Hence “linear” discriminant analysis. With equal priors, the boundary between two classes is the midpoint \((\mu_0 + \mu_1)/2\).

The estimates are all averages (Toolkit 2): \(\hat\pi_k = n_k/n\), \(\hat\mu_k\) = class mean, \(\hat\sigma^2 = \frac{1}{n - m}\sum_k\sum_{i\in k}(x_i - \hat\mu_k)^2\) (pooled, \(m\) classes).

4. The Gaussian as a kernel

In kernel density estimation (w7) each data point puts down a bump and the estimate is the average bump height:

\[f_{\text{KDE}}(x) = \frac{1}{nh^p}\sum_{i=1}^{n}K\Big(\frac{x - x_i}{h}\Big), \qquad K(u) = (2\pi)^{-p/2}e^{-\frac12u^Tu}.\]

\(K\) is a Gaussian with mean 0 and variance 1 in each direction, and \(u^Tu\) is a squared length (Toolkit 1). The bandwidth \(h\) plays the role of \(\sigma\): wider bumps mean a smoother estimate (Toolkit 5).

In one dimension, \(K(u) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}e^{-u^2/2}\). Compute \(K(1)\).

\(0.3989 \times e^{-0.5} = 0.3989 \times 0.6065 = 0.242\). Same as the worked example: \(K\) is the Gaussian with \(\mu = 0\), \(\sigma = 1\).

Stage test

LDA, one input, two classes with equal priors, class means \(\hat\mu_0 = 1\) and \(\hat\mu_1 = 7\), shared variance. Where is the decision boundary?

With equal priors the \(\log\pi_k\) terms cancel and the boundary is the midpoint: \((1 + 7)/2 = 4\).

Why is LDA’s decision boundary linear in \(x\)?

If each class had its own variance (QDA), the \(x^2\) terms wouldn’t cancel and the boundary would be quadratic.

Under which assumption is minimising RSS the same as maximum likelihood?

The log of a Gaussian density is a constant minus a squared error, so the log-likelihood is a constant minus RSS/\((2\sigma^2)\).
