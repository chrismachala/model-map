Fit plus penalty, and flexibility knobs

A flexible model can always fit the training data better, and that's the trap. The course's answer, in week after week, is one formula: minimise (how badly you fit) + (a dial) × (how complicated you are). Ridge, tree pruning and penalised logistic regression are the same sentence with different words filled in.

Book and slides

ISLP 6.2.1 (ridge), 8.1.1 “Tree pruning” (cost-complexity, eq. 8.4), 10.7.2 (regularisation and early stopping in networks), 2.2.2 (flexibility and the U-shape). Skip ISLP 6.2.2 (the lasso) beyond knowing its name and that it uses \(\sum\lvert\beta_j\rvert\); the slides only name it. Skip 6.1 (subset selection) and 6.3 (PCR, PLS). Week 5 slides present regularisation as “fit + penalty” across models.

1. The one formula

\[\boxed{\text{minimise}\quad \underbrace{\text{loss on training data}}_{\text{fit}} + \underbrace{\lambda \times \text{complexity}}_{\text{penalty}}}\]

  • \(\lambda = 0\): no penalty, the most flexible fit (risk: high variance, overfitting).
  • \(\lambda\) huge: the penalty dominates, the simplest possible model (risk: high bias, underfitting).
  • In between: chosen on a validation set or by cross-validation, never on the training data (where \(\lambda = 0\) would always win).
Model Loss Complexity Dial
Ridge regression (w2) RSS \(\|\beta\|_2^2 = \sum_{j\ge1}\beta_j^2\) (not \(\beta_0\)) \(\lambda\)
Penalised logistic regression (w3 lab Q2) NLL \(\|\beta\|_2^2\) \(\lambda\)
Cost-complexity pruning (w4) \(\text{RSS}(T)\) or misclassification \(\lvert T\rvert\), the number of leaves \(\alpha\) (ccp_alpha)
Neural network weight decay (w4, ISLP 10.7) RSS or cross-entropy \(\sum w^2\) \(\lambda\)

Worked example. Two candidate lines: A has RSS \(10\) and \(\beta = (3, 1)\) (so \(\|\beta\|^2 = 10\)); B has RSS \(14\) and \(\beta = (1, 1)\) (\(\|\beta\|^2 = 2\)). With \(\lambda = 1\): A scores \(20\), B scores \(16\), so ridge prefers B despite its worse fit. With \(\lambda = 0.1\): A \(11\), B \(14.2\), so A wins.

2. Why the penalty works: shrinking

With one centred input, ridge has a closed form (set the derivative to zero, Toolkit 4):

\[\hat\beta^{\text{ridge}} = \frac{\sum x_iy_i}{\sum x_i^2 + \lambda}.\]

The \(+\lambda\) in the denominator pulls the coefficient towards 0. Smaller coefficients mean predictions that change less when the data change: lower variance, at the price of a little bias.

3. Pruning is the same formula

\[C_\alpha(T) = \text{RSS}(T) + \alpha\lvert T\rvert.\]

Each leaf costs a fee \(\alpha\). A split is worth keeping only if it lowers the RSS by more than \(\alpha\). As \(\alpha\) grows, the best subtree shrinks.

Subtrees of a big tree: 1 leaf, RSS 100; 2 leaves, RSS 60; 3 leaves, RSS 50; 5 leaves, RSS 44. With \(\alpha = 12\), how many leaves does the subtree minimising \(\text{RSS} + \alpha\lvert T\rvert\) have?

\(100 + 12 = 112\); \(60 + 24 = 84\); \(50 + 36 = 86\); \(44 + 60 = 104\). Minimum \(84\), at 2 leaves.

4. Not every knob is a penalty

Some models control flexibility without a penalty term. Know the knob for each model, because “how do you control overfitting in X?” is asked every year:

Model The knob More flexible when
KNN (w1, w2) \(K\) \(K\) smaller
Polynomial regression (w2) degree \(d\) \(d\) bigger
Step functions (w2) number of cut points more cuts
Ridge (w2) \(\lambda\) \(\lambda\) smaller
Trees (w4) depth / leaves; \(\alpha\) deeper; \(\alpha\) smaller
Neural networks (w4) hidden units, layers, epochs (early stopping), weight decay more units, longer training
Bagging, random forests (w6) \(B\) more \(B\) does not overfit

Kernel density (w7) bandwidth \(h\) \(h\) smaller (spikier)
K-means (w7) number of clusters \(K\) \(K\) bigger (SSE always falls)

Boosting’s \(\eta\) is a regulariser of a different kind: instead of penalising size, it adds each new tree at only a fraction of its strength, so the model learns slowly and is less likely to chase noise.

KNN with \(K = 1\) has training error 0 but a high test error. Which change is most likely to help, and why?

Small \(K\) is the most flexible end of KNN. Raising \(K\) trades a little bias for a big drop in variance, until the U-shape bottoms out.

Stage test

(4 marks, exam format 3.) Explain what “regularisation” means, using two different models from the course as examples.

Regularisation means deliberately limiting a model’s flexibility so it fits the signal rather than the noise, usually by minimising training loss plus a penalty on complexity. In ridge regression the objective is \(\text{RSS} + \lambda\sum_{j\ge1}\beta_j^2\): the penalty shrinks the coefficients towards zero, lowering variance at the cost of some bias. In cost-complexity pruning of a tree the objective is \(\text{RSS}(T) + \alpha\lvert T\rvert\), which charges a fee per leaf so that only splits that reduce the RSS by more than \(\alpha\) survive. In both cases the dial (\(\lambda\) or \(\alpha\)) is chosen on a validation set or by cross-validation, because on the training data no penalty would always look best.

Which ensemble method can overfit if you use too many trees \(B\)?

Bagging and forests average independent-ish trees, so more trees only reduce variance. Boosting keeps fitting the residuals, so with enough trees it fits the noise (2025 Q3a).
