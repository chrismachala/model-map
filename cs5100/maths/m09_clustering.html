K-means and hierarchical clustering

No labels now, but the same tools. K-means is distances, means and squared error, minimised by alternating two easy steps. Hierarchical clustering is distances between points, turned into distances between groups.

Built from

A1 Euclidean and other distances · A2 the centroid (mean) · A3 the SSE and “the mean minimises squared error” · A4 alternating minimisation, local minima, restarts · A5 the number of clusters \(K\) as a knob. Full pages: K-means, hierarchical clustering; week-order stages: 23 and 24.

1. K-means: the assembly

\[J_{SSE} = \sum_{j=1}^{K}\sum_{x\in\omega_j}\underbrace{\|x - \mu_j\|^2}_{\text{A1, A3}}\]

Repeat until nothing changes:

  1. Assign each point to its nearest centre (A1). This can only lower \(J\), because each point picks its smallest term.
  2. Update each centre to the mean of its points (A2). This can only lower \(J\), because the mean minimises squared distance (A3).

\(J\) never rises and there are finitely many assignments, so it stops, but possibly in a local minimum (A4): run several random starts and keep the lowest \(J\). \(J\) always falls as \(K\) grows, so \(K\) is chosen by an elbow or by purpose, not by \(J\) alone (A5).

2. Hierarchical clustering: distances between groups

Start with every point as a cluster; repeatedly merge the two closest clusters. “Closest” needs a distance between groups, built from point distances (A1):

Linkage Distance between clusters \(A\) and \(B\)
Single smallest \(d(a, b)\)
Complete largest \(d(a, b)\)
Average mean of all \(d(a, b)\) (A2)
Centroid \(d(\bar a, \bar b)\), distance between the means (A2)

The merge heights form the dendrogram; cut it at a height to get clusters. Single linkage tends to chain; centroid linkage can produce inversions.

Stage test

(4 marks; 2023 Q5.) Describe the K-means algorithm and explain why it always terminates. Does it always find the best clustering?

K-means seeks \(K\) clusters minimising \(J = \sum_{j=1}^K\sum_{x\in\omega_j}\|x - \mu_j\|^2\). Starting from \(K\) initial centres, it repeats two steps: assign each point to its nearest centre, then replace each centre by the mean of the points assigned to it. The assignment step cannot increase \(J\) (each point moves to its closest centre) and the update step cannot increase \(J\) (the mean minimises the sum of squared distances). Since \(J\) never increases and there are only finitely many ways to partition the data, the algorithm must stop. It converges to a local minimum that depends on the initial centres, so in practice it is run from several random starts and the solution with the smallest \(J\) is kept.
