Density estimation

Where do the data crowd together? Estimate the probability density by counting how many points fall in a small window around x and dividing by n times the window's size. Parzen windows use a box; kernel density estimation uses smooth bumps. The window width h is the knob.

Question type: unsupervised: estimate the density \(f(x)\) the data came from.
Week: 7 (slides 51–72).
Book: not in ISLP. Optional: Duda, Hart and Stork 4.2–4.3 (Parzen windows); Bishop 2.5 (kernel density estimators). This page covers everything on the slides.
Past paper: 2023 Q1(d) (is a Gaussian mixture parametric? the curse of dimensionality for histograms, 6 marks); q07 Q31–42.
Techniques on this page: histograms, \(k/(nV)\), Parzen windows by hand, smooth kernels, bandwidth.

The problem it solves

Knowing \(f\) tells you where data are likely: use it to classify (compare class densities), spot outliers (tiny density), or re-weight training data. Parametric methods assume a shape (a Gaussian, a mixture of Gaussians) and fit its parameters. Non-parametric methods, the ones on the slides, let the data decide the shape.

Words you’ll hear

Word Plain meaning
density \(f(x)\) probability per unit length (area, volume) near \(x\)
histogram count per bin, divided by \(n \times\) bin width
Parzen window a box of side \(h\) centred at \(x\); count the points inside
kernel \(K\) the shape of each point’s bump; integrates to 1
bandwidth \(h\) the window width: the smoothing knob
curse of dimensionality number of bins (and data needed) grows exponentially with \(p\)

Technique 1: histograms and their problems (slides 53–55)

\(f_H(x) = \frac{\#\{x_i \text{ in } x\text{'s bin}\}}{n\,w}\). Parameters: number of bins, width, starting position. Problems: the shape depends on where bins start; it jumps at bin edges; and the curse of dimensionality: \(m\) bins per axis in \(p\) dimensions is \(m^p\) bins. 10 per axis in 10 dimensions is \(10^{10}\) bins, so even a million points leave nearly all of them empty.

Technique 2: \(f(x) \approx k/(nV)\) (slides 56–59)

Region \(R\) around \(x\), volume \(V\). \(P(\text{point in } R) = \int_Rf \approx f(x)V\) when \(R\) is small, and \(P \approx k/n\) by relative frequency. So \[f(x) \approx \frac{k}{nV}.\] \(V\) big enough to hold enough points (variance), small enough that \(f\) is flat inside (bias).

Technique 3: Parzen windows by hand (slides 60–63)

Box of side \(h\) centred at \(x\), \(V = h^p\), \(K(u) = 1\) if every \(|u_j| \le \frac12\): \[f(x) = \frac{1}{nh^p}\sum_{i=1}^nK\Big(\frac{x - x_i}{h}\Big).\] In 1D: count the points within \(h/2\) of \(x\) (edges count), divide by \(nh\).

Slides 62–63. \(X = \{4, 5, 5, 6, 12, 14, 15, 15, 16, 17\}\), \(h = 4\), so \(nh = 40\). \(x = 3\): \([1, 5]\) holds 4, 5, 5: \(3/40 = \mathbf{0.075}\). \(x = 10\): \([8, 12]\) holds 12: \(\mathbf{0.025}\). \(x = 15\): \([13, 17]\) holds five: \(\mathbf{0.125}\).

2D (q07-style). \(h = 2\) at \((0, 0)\); data \((0.5, 0.5), (1, -0.9), (1.5, 0), (-3, 2)\). Inside \(|x_1|, |x_2| \le 1\): the first two. \(f = 2/(4 \times 2^2) = 0.125\).

Technique 4: smooth kernels and bandwidth (slides 64–71)

The box makes \(f\) jump, and weights a point at the edge the same as one at the centre. Swap in a smooth kernel (Gaussian \(\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}e^{-u^2/2}\), triangular \(1 - |u|\), Epanechnikov…) in the same formula: KDE, a sum of \(n\) bumps of area \(1/n\).

q07 Q41(b), triangular, \(h = 3\), \(x = 4.5\). Data \((1, 2, 4, 4, 4, 5, 6, 8, 8, 8)\). \(u = (x_i - 4.5)/3\); weights \(1 - |u|\): point 2 gives 0.167, each 4 and the 5 give 0.833, 6 gives 0.5, others 0. Sum 4.0; \(f = 4.0/30 = \mathbf{0.133}\).

Bandwidth matters more than kernel shape. Large \(h\): over-smoothed, peaks merge (bias). Small \(h\): a spike per point (variance). Choose by rule of thumb or cross-validation.

q07 Q41’s hint writes \(K\big(\frac{x_i - x}{h}\big)\); the slides write \(K\big(\frac{x - x_i}{h}\big)\). Same thing for symmetric kernels. Slide 55’s “Chapter 8” is a later chapter of this course, not ISLP’s.
