Probability and Bayes' rule

Classification is about one quantity: the probability of each class given the inputs, P(Y = k | X = x). The Bayes classifier wants it, KNN estimates it by counting, logistic regression models it directly, and LDA and naive Bayes get it by flipping the other conditional with Bayes' rule. The same few probability facts give the bootstrap's 36.8% and the sign test's p-value. This stage does every calculation with a counting table first, so the formulas have something solid to stand on.

By the end of this stage you can

  • read and compute a conditional probability, and tell \(P(A\mid B)\) apart from \(P(B\mid A)\);
  • apply Bayes’ rule using a “imagine 10,000 people” table, then with the formula;
  • compute posteriors \(P(Y = k\mid x)\) from priors and class densities, and the Bayes classifier’s prediction and error;
  • do a naive Bayes calculation by multiplying per-input probabilities;
  • compute \((1 - 1/n)^n\) and a one-sided binomial p-value.

Book and slides

Skip 4.4.2 to 4.4.3’s multivariate Gaussian algebra; the slides do one input. Week 5 slides for the binomial sign test; week 6 slide 24 for the \(1/e\) lemma and its proof.

1. Conditional probability

In plain words

\(P(A\mid B)\) means: look only at the cases where \(B\) happened, and ask what fraction of those also have \(A\). The bar “|” means “among those with”. The order matters: “the fraction of smokers among people with lung disease” is not “the fraction with lung disease among smokers”.

\[\boxed{P(A\mid B) = \frac{P(A\text{ and }B)}{P(B)}}\]

Independent events: \(P(A\text{ and }B) = P(A)\,P(B)\), equivalently \(P(A\mid B) = P(A)\). Knowing \(B\) tells you nothing about \(A\).

Worked example 1: from a table of counts. 200 emails:

contains “win” no “win” total
spam 30 50 80
ham 10 110 120
total 40 160 200
  • \(P(\text{win}\mid\text{spam})\): among the 80 spam, 30 have “win”: \(30/80 = 0.375\).
  • \(P(\text{spam}\mid\text{win})\): among the 40 with “win”, 30 are spam: \(30/40 = 0.75\).

Same 30 on top; a different “among” underneath. Those are the two directions Bayes’ rule converts between.

Common mistakes

  • Flipping the conditional. \(P(\text{positive test}\mid\text{ill})\) is the test’s sensitivity; \(P(\text{ill}\mid\text{positive})\) is what a patient wants. They can be wildly different.
  • Dividing by the grand total instead of the “among” group.

Using the table above, give \(P(\text{win}\mid\text{ham})\) and \(P(\text{ham}\mid\text{win})\).

Among the 120 ham, 10 have “win”: \(10/120 = 0.0833\). Among the 40 with “win”, 10 are ham: \(10/40 = 0.25\).

2. Bayes’ rule

In plain words

Bayes’ rule turns “how likely is the evidence if the class is \(k\)?” (easy to estimate, one class at a time) into “how likely is class \(k\) given the evidence?” (what you actually want). Multiply each class’s prior by how well it explains the evidence, then rescale so the answers add to 1.

\[\boxed{P(Y = k\mid X = x) = \frac{\pi_k\,f_k(x)}{\sum_{l}\pi_l\,f_l(x)}}\]

Symbol Meaning
\(\pi_k = P(Y = k)\) the prior: how common class \(k\) is before looking at \(x\)
\(f_k(x)\) the class density / likelihood: how probable input \(x\) is within class \(k\)
\(\pi_kf_k(x)\) the class’s score: prior × likelihood
denominator the sum of all class scores; it makes the posteriors add to 1
\(P(Y = k\mid x)\) the posterior

Worked example 2: the rare disease, by counting. 1% of people have a disease. The test is positive for 90% of the ill and 5% of the healthy. Imagine 10,000 people:

test + test − total
ill 10,000 × 0.01 × 0.9 = 90 10 100
healthy 9,900 × 0.05 = 495 9,405 9,900
total 585 9,415 10,000

Among the 585 positives, 90 are ill: \(P(\text{ill}\mid +) = 90/585 = 0.154\).

Same thing with the formula: \(\dfrac{0.01\times0.9}{0.01\times0.9 + 0.99\times0.05} = \dfrac{0.009}{0.0585} = 0.154\).

A positive test still means only a 15% chance, because healthy people vastly outnumber ill ones. The prior matters.

Common mistakes

  • Answering 0.9. That’s \(P(+\mid\text{ill})\), the other direction.
  • Leaving out the healthy positives in the denominator. The bottom line is every way to test positive.
  • Using \(\pi\) for the healthy class too. The healthy prior is \(1 - \pi\).

Try it with me. 5% of transactions are fraud. A detector flags 80% of frauds and 10% of genuine transactions. Imagine 1,000 transactions.

How many are frauds, how many frauds get flagged, and how many genuine ones get flagged?

\(1000\times0.05 = 50\) frauds; \(50\times0.8 = 40\) flagged. \(950\) genuine; \(950\times0.1 = 95\) flagged.

Now: what fraction of flagged transactions are fraud?

\(40/(40 + 95) = 40/135 = 0.296\). Formula: \(\frac{0.05\times0.8}{0.05\times0.8 + 0.95\times0.1} = \frac{0.04}{0.135}\).

3. The Bayes classifier: the ideal

In plain words

If you knew the posteriors exactly, the best possible rule is: predict the class with the biggest posterior. Its error at \(x\) is “1 minus that biggest posterior”. No classifier can beat it on average: its error rate is the floor, the Bayes error rate.

\[\hat Y(x) = \arg\max_kP(Y = k\mid X = x),\qquad \text{error at }x = 1 - \max_kP(Y = k\mid X = x)\]

To predict, you can skip the denominator: \(\arg\max_k\pi_kf_k(x)\) picks the same class, since every posterior has the same denominator.

Worked example 3. Two classes, priors \(\pi_0 = 0.7\), \(\pi_1 = 0.3\). At some \(x_0\), \(f_0(x_0) = 0.2\) and \(f_1(x_0) = 0.6\).

class prior \(\pi_k\) \(f_k(x_0)\) score \(\pi_kf_k\) posterior
0 0.7 0.2 0.14 0.14/0.32 = 0.4375
1 0.3 0.6 0.18 0.18/0.32 = 0.5625
total 0.32 1

Predict class 1 (biggest score, so biggest posterior). Error at \(x_0\): \(1 - 0.5625 = 0.4375\). Note class 1 wins even though it is rarer: its density at \(x_0\) is three times higher.

How each classifier gets the posteriors:

Classifier How it gets \(P(Y = k\mid x)\)
KNN (w1) count labels among the \(K\) neighbours (Toolkit 2)
Logistic regression (w3) models it directly: \(\sigma(\beta\cdot\tilde x)\) or softmax (Toolkit 8)
LDA (w3) Gaussian \(f_k\), then Bayes’ rule (Toolkit 7)
Naive Bayes (w3) \(f_k\) as a product over inputs, then Bayes’ rule
Trees (w4) class proportions in the leaf
NN classifier (w4) softmax of the outputs

Priors \(\pi_0 = 0.5\), \(\pi_1 = 0.5\). At \(x_0\), \(f_0 = 0.3\), \(f_1 = 0.4\). Give both scores, \(P(Y = 0\mid x_0)\) and the predicted class.

Scores \(0.15\) and \(0.20\), total \(0.35\). \(P(Y = 0\mid x_0) = 0.15/0.35 = 0.4286\). Predict class 1.

4. Naive Bayes: multiply the inputs

In plain words

With many inputs, the joint density \(f_k(x_1, \dots, x_p)\) is hard to estimate. Naive Bayes pretends the inputs are independent within each class, so the joint is just the product of one-input probabilities, each easy to count.

\[f_k(x) = f_{k1}(x_1)\times f_{k2}(x_2)\times\dots\times f_{kp}(x_p)\]

Worked example 4: spam filter. \(\pi_{\text{spam}} = 0.4\). The email contains “win” and “free”.

class prior \(P(\text{win}\mid\cdot)\) \(P(\text{free}\mid\cdot)\) score = product
spam 0.4 0.5 0.6 0.4 × 0.5 × 0.6 = 0.12
ham 0.6 0.1 0.2 0.6 × 0.1 × 0.2 = 0.012
total 0.132

\(P(\text{spam}\mid x) = 0.12/0.132 = 0.909\).

Common mistakes

  • Adding instead of multiplying the per-word probabilities.
  • Forgetting the prior in each row.
  • “Naive” means independent within each class, not “equal variances” (that’s LDA) and not “ignores the prior”.

5. Repeated independent trials

In plain words

For independent events, “all of them happen” means multiply. That gives two results the course uses.

The bootstrap’s 36.8% (w6). Draw \(n\) times with replacement from \(n\) points. One draw misses a given point with probability \(1 - \frac1n\). All \(n\) draws miss it with probability

\[\Big(1 - \frac1n\Big)^n \;\to\; e^{-1} \approx 0.368.\]

\(n\) \((1 - 1/n)^n\)
2 \(0.5^2 = 0.25\)
4 \(0.75^4 = 0.316\)
5 \(0.8^5 = 0.328\)
10 \(0.9^{10} = 0.349\)
1000 \(0.368\)

So about a third of the points are left out of each bootstrap sample (“out of bag”).

The sign test (w5). Two classifiers disagree on \(N\) test points. If they’re equally good, each disagreement goes to A with probability \(\frac12\), independently, so \(Y\) = “wins for A” is Binomial\((N, \frac12)\):

\[P(Y = j) = \binom{N}{j}\frac{1}{2^N},\qquad p\text{-value} = P(Y \ge k).\]

Worked example 5. A is right on 10 of \(N = 12\) disagreements.

\(j\) \(\binom{12}{j}\)
10 66
11 12
12 1
total 79

\(p = 79/2^{12} = 79/4096 = 0.0193\). Below 0.05, so reject “equally good” at the 5% level (one-sided).

Common mistakes

  • Using all test points for \(N\). Only the points where the two classifiers disagree count.
  • \(P(Y = k)\) instead of \(P(Y \ge k)\). The p-value is the probability of a result at least this extreme: add the tail.
  • \(\binom{N}{j}\) slips. \(\binom{N}{N} = 1\), \(\binom{N}{N-1} = N\), \(\binom{N}{N-2} = N(N-1)/2\).

Two classifiers disagree on 10 points; A wins 8. Give the three binomial coefficients you need and the p-value \(P(Y \ge 8)\).

\(\binom{10}{8} = 45\), \(\binom{10}{9} = 10\), \(\binom{10}{10} = 1\). \(p = 56/1024 = 0.0547\). Not below 0.05, so not significant at 5%.

Stage test

Two classes. At \(x_0\) the true \(P(Y = 1\mid x_0) = 0.25\). What is the Bayes error rate at \(x_0\)?

Bayes predicts class 0 (probability 0.75) and errs with probability \(0.25\).

A test has \(P(+\mid\text{ill}) = 0.99\). Which statement is right?

\(P(\text{ill}\mid +) = \frac{\pi\times0.99}{\pi\times0.99 + (1 - \pi)P(+\mid\text{healthy})}\): it needs the prior and the false-positive rate.
