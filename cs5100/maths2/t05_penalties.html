Fit plus penalty, and flexibility knobs

A flexible model can always fit the training data better, and that's the trap. The course's answer, week after week, is one formula: minimise (how badly you fit) + (a dial) × (how complicated you are). Ridge, tree pruning and penalised logistic regression are the same sentence with different words filled in. This stage computes those objectives by table and shows exactly how the dial changes the winner.

By the end of this stage you can

  • write the “loss + λ × complexity” objective for ridge, penalised logistic regression and pruning;
  • compute and compare penalised objectives for several candidate models, and say which one wins for a given λ;
  • compute a one-input ridge coefficient and see the shrinkage;
  • choose the best pruned subtree for a given α, and find the α where the choice changes;
  • name the flexibility knob of every model in the course, and which direction means “more flexible”.

Book and slides

Skip 6.2.2 (the lasso) beyond its name and its penalty \(\sum\lvert\beta_j\rvert\); the slides only name it. Skip 6.1 (subset selection) and 6.3 (PCR, PLS). Week 2 slides end at ridge; week 5 slides present pruning and ridge as “fit + penalty”. Lab note: scikit-learn’s ccp_alpha is the slides’ \(\alpha\) divided by \(n\), because sklearn’s impurities are MSEs, not RSSs.

1. The one formula

In plain words

Think of a job interview with two scores: how well the candidate answered (fit) and how much they’d cost (complexity). The dial \(\lambda\) says how much you care about cost. Dial at 0: hire whoever answered best, whatever the cost. Dial huge: hire the cheapest. Somewhere in between gives the best long-term choice, and you find it by testing on new data (validation), not the interview itself.

\[\boxed{\text{minimise}\quad\underbrace{\text{loss on training data}}_{\text{fit}} + \underbrace{\lambda\times\text{complexity}}_{\text{penalty}}}\]

Model Loss Complexity Dial
Ridge regression (w2) RSS \(\|\beta\|_2^2 = \sum_{j\ge1}\beta_j^2\) (not \(\beta_0\)) \(\lambda\)
Penalised logistic regression (w3 lab Q2) NLL \(\sum_{j\ge1}\beta_j^2\) \(\lambda\)
Cost-complexity pruning (w4) \(\text{RSS}(T)\) (or misclassification) \(\lvert T\rvert\), number of leaves \(\alpha\)
Network weight decay (w4, ISLP 10.7) RSS or cross-entropy \(\sum w^2\) \(\lambda\)

Worked example 1: which model wins? Three fitted lines (penalty excludes \(\beta_0\)):

model RSS \((\beta_1, \beta_2)\) \(\|\beta\|^2 = \beta_1^2 + \beta_2^2\)
A 10 (3, 1) 9 + 1 = 10
B 14 (1, 1) 1 + 1 = 2
C 25 (0, 0) 0

Objective \(= \text{RSS} + \lambda\|\beta\|^2\) for three dials:

model \(\lambda = 0.1\) \(\lambda = 1\) \(\lambda = 10\)
A 10 + 1 = 11 10 + 10 = 20 10 + 100 = 110
B 14 + 0.2 = 14.2 14 + 2 = 16 14 + 20 = 34
C 25 25 25 + 0 = 25

Small \(\lambda\) picks the best fit (A); medium picks the compromise (B); large picks the simplest (C). Same data, three answers: that’s why \(\lambda\) must be chosen on validation data.

Common mistakes

  • Penalising the intercept. Ridge’s sum starts at \(j = 1\). The intercept just sets the average level; shrinking it would bias every prediction.
  • Forgetting to square. \(\|\beta\|_2^2 = 3^2 + 1^2 = 10\), not \(3 + 1 = 4\) (that would be the lasso’s \(\sum\lvert\beta_j\rvert\)).
  • Choosing \(\lambda\) on training data. \(\lambda = 0\) always minimises the training RSS, so training data can’t choose it.

Ridge, \(\lambda = 2\). Model A: RSS 9, \((\beta_1, \beta_2) = (2, 0)\). Model B: RSS 10, \((\beta_1, \beta_2) = (0.5, 0.5)\). Give each model’s penalty \(\lambda\|\beta\|^2\) and its objective. (Which wins?)

A: \(\|\beta\|^2 = 4\), penalty \(8\), objective \(17\). B: \(\|\beta\|^2 = 0.25 + 0.25 = 0.5\), penalty \(1\), objective \(10 + 1 = 11\). B wins: its slightly worse fit is outweighed by much smaller coefficients.

2. Why the penalty helps: shrinking

In plain words

With the penalty, every coefficient has to “earn its keep”: it only grows if that lowers the RSS by more than it costs. Coefficients get pulled towards 0. Smaller coefficients mean predictions that change less when the training data change, so the variance drops. The price is a little bias.

With one centred input (so \(\bar x = 0\) and no intercept to worry about), set the derivative of \(\sum_i(y_i - \beta x_i)^2 + \lambda\beta^2\) to zero:

Step Working
derivative \(-2\sum_ix_i(y_i - \beta x_i) + 2\lambda\beta\)
set to 0 \(\sum_ix_iy_i = \beta\big(\sum_ix_i^2 + \lambda\big)\)
solve \(\hat\beta^{\text{ridge}} = \dfrac{\sum_ix_iy_i}{\sum_ix_i^2 + \lambda}\)

\(\lambda = 0\) gives ordinary least squares; as \(\lambda \to \infty\), \(\hat\beta \to 0\).

Worked example 2. Centred \(x = (-2, -1, 1, 2)\), \(y = (-3, -1, 2, 4)\).

\(x_i\) \(y_i\) \(x_iy_i\) \(x_i^2\)
−2 −3 6 4
−1 −1 1 1
1 2 2 1
2 4 8 4
total 17 10
\(\lambda\) \(\hat\beta = 17/(10 + \lambda)\)
0 1.7 (least squares)
5 17/15 = 1.133
10 17/20 = 0.85
100 17/110 = 0.155

The coefficient shrinks smoothly towards 0 but never reaches it.

3. Pruning is the same formula

In plain words

For trees, “complexity” is the number of leaves. Each leaf costs a fee \(\alpha\). A split survives only if it lowers the RSS by more than the fee.

\[C_\alpha(T) = \text{RSS}(T) + \alpha\lvert T\rvert\]

Worked example 3. The pruning sequence of a big tree, and the objective \(\text{RSS} + \alpha\lvert T\rvert\) for four values of \(\alpha\) (smallest in each column in bold):

subtree leaves \(\lvert T\rvert\) RSS \(\alpha = 2\) \(\alpha = 5\) \(\alpha = 12\) \(\alpha = 50\)
\(T_1\) 1 100 102 105 112 150
\(T_2\) 2 60 64 70 84 160
\(T_3\) 3 50 56 65 86 200
\(T_5\) 5 44 54 69 104 294

As \(\alpha\) grows the winner gets smaller: \(T_5\), then \(T_3\), then \(T_2\), then \(T_1\).

Where does the choice switch? Compare neighbours. \(T_3\) beats \(T_5\) when \(50 + 3\alpha < 44 + 5\alpha\), i.e. \(\alpha > 3\). \(T_2\) beats \(T_3\) when \(60 + 2\alpha < 50 + 3\alpha\), i.e. \(\alpha > 10\). \(T_1\) beats \(T_2\) when \(100 + \alpha < 60 + 2\alpha\), i.e. \(\alpha > 40\). Each switch point is “RSS saved ÷ leaves added”: \(6/2 = 3\), \(10/1 = 10\), \(40/1 = 40\).

Common mistakes

  • Comparing RSS alone. The biggest tree always has the smallest RSS; you must add \(\alpha\lvert T\rvert\).
  • Counting all nodes instead of leaves (terminal nodes) for \(\lvert T\rvert\).
  • sklearn’s ccp_alpha is the slides’ \(\alpha\) divided by \(n\).

Subtrees: 1 leaf RSS 100; 2 leaves RSS 60; 3 leaves RSS 50; 5 leaves RSS 44. With \(\alpha = 12\), give the objective for the 2-leaf and 3-leaf trees, and the number of leaves of the best subtree.

\(60 + 24 = 84\); \(50 + 36 = 86\). Also \(100 + 12 = 112\) and \(44 + 60 = 104\). Best: 2 leaves.

4. Not every knob is a penalty

“How do you control overfitting in X?” is asked every year. Know the knob, and which way is “more flexible”:

Model The knob More flexible when
KNN (w1, w2) \(K\) \(K\) smaller
Polynomial regression (w2) degree \(d\) \(d\) bigger
Step functions (w2) number of cut points more cuts
Ridge (w2) \(\lambda\) \(\lambda\) smaller
Trees (w4) depth or leaves; \(\alpha\) deeper; \(\alpha\) smaller
Neural networks (w4) units, layers, epochs, weight decay more units, longer training, less decay
Bagging, random forests (w6) \(B\) more \(B\) does not overfit
Boosting (w6) \(B\), depth \(d\), shrinkage \(\eta\) bigger \(B\) can overfit; bigger \(\eta\) learns faster
Kernel density (w7) bandwidth \(h\) \(h\) smaller (spikier)
K-means (w7) number of clusters \(K\) \(K\) bigger (SSE always falls)

KNN with \(K = 1\) has training error 0 but a high test error. Which change is most likely to help, and why?

\(K = 1\) is the most flexible end. Raising \(K\) trades a little bias for a big drop in variance.

Stage test

(4 marks, exam format 3.) Explain what “regularisation” means, using two different models from the course as examples.

Regularisation means limiting a model’s flexibility so it fits the signal rather than the noise, usually by minimising training loss plus a penalty on complexity. In ridge regression the objective is \(\text{RSS} + \lambda\sum_{j\ge1}\beta_j^2\): the penalty shrinks coefficients towards zero, lowering variance at the cost of some bias. In cost-complexity pruning the objective is \(\text{RSS}(T) + \alpha\lvert T\rvert\), which charges a fee per leaf so only splits that reduce the RSS by more than \(\alpha\) survive. In both cases the dial is chosen on a validation set or by cross-validation, because on the training data no penalty always looks best.

Which ensemble method can overfit if you use too many trees \(B\)?

Bagging and forests average many trees, so more trees only reduce variance. Boosting keeps fitting the residuals, so with enough trees it fits the noise (2025 Q3a).
