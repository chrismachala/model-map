Variance, covariance and projections

Week 8 adds one more piece. Spread in one direction is a variance; how two attributes move together is a covariance; collect them all and you get the covariance matrix. Projecting the data onto a direction is a dot product, and the spread along that direction has a neat formula. The directions of largest spread are the principal components. This stage computes every one of those by table.

By the end of this stage you can

  • centre a data set and compute variances and covariances (with \(\frac1n\), as the slides do);
  • assemble the covariance matrix \(\Sigma\) and the scatter matrix \(S = n\Sigma\);
  • project points onto a unit direction and compute the variance of the projections two ways;
  • find the eigenvalues of a \(2\times2\) covariance matrix and read them as “spread along the best directions”;
  • compute a proportion of variance explained.

Book and slides

ISLP 12.2.1 (eq. 12.1 to 12.3: normalised linear combinations and their sample variance). Week 8 slides 21 to 27. The slides use \(\frac1n\) throughout; scikit-learn and pandas use \(\frac1{n - 1}\), which scales the numbers by \(\frac{n}{n - 1}\) but doesn’t change the directions. The general definition of eigenvectors is optional (slide 26).

1. Centring, variance and covariance

In plain words

Variance = how spread out one attribute is: the average squared gap from its mean (Toolkit 3 again). Covariance = do two attributes rise and fall together? Multiply their gaps from the mean point by point and average: same-sign gaps (both above or both below) give positive products.

Centring means subtracting each column’s mean, so every column then has mean 0. After centring, the gaps are the values.

\[\text{Var}(x_j) = \frac1n\sum_i(x_{ij} - \mu_j)^2,\qquad \text{Cov}(x_j, x_k) = \frac1n\sum_i(x_{ij} - \mu_j)(x_{ik} - \mu_k)\]

Worked example 1. Raw data \((4, 3)\), \((2, 5)\), \((0, -1)\), \((-2, 1)\).

Step 1: centre. Means \(\mu_1 = (4 + 2 + 0 - 2)/4 = 1\), \(\mu_2 = (3 + 5 - 1 + 1)/4 = 2\).

raw centred \((x_1 - 1, x_2 - 2)\) \(x_1^2\) \(x_2^2\) \(x_1x_2\)
(4, 3) (3, 1) 9 1 3
(2, 5) (1, 3) 1 9 3
(0, −1) (−1, −3) 1 9 3
(−2, 1) (−3, −1) 9 1 3
total (0, 0) 20 20 12

Step 2: divide by \(n = 4\). \(\text{Var}(x_1) = 5\), \(\text{Var}(x_2) = 5\), \(\text{Cov}(x_1, x_2) = 3\). Positive: the two attributes tend to rise together.

Common mistakes

  • Forgetting to centre. Squaring raw values measures spread around 0, not around the mean. (The same slip as the K-means SSE.)
  • \(\frac1{n-1}\) vs \(\frac1n\). The slides use \(\frac1n\). Say which you use.
  • Covariance sign. Keep the signs of both gaps: \((-1)(-3) = +3\).

2. The covariance matrix

\[\boxed{\Sigma = \frac1nX^TX\quad\text{(for centred }X\text{)},\qquad S = n\Sigma}\]

Variances on the diagonal, covariances off it. It’s symmetric: \(\Sigma_{12} = \Sigma_{21}\). From worked example 1:

\[\Sigma = \begin{pmatrix}5 & 3\\3 & 5\end{pmatrix},\qquad S = \begin{pmatrix}20 & 12\\12 & 20\end{pmatrix}.\]

Standardising (dividing each centred column by its standard deviation) turns \(\Sigma\) into the correlation matrix, with 1s on the diagonal. Here: \(\text{corr} = 3/\sqrt{5\times5} = 0.6\).

Try it with me. Centred data \((2, 1)\), \((-2, -1)\), \((1, 3)\), \((-1, -3)\).

Give the column sums of \(x_1^2\), \(x_2^2\) and \(x_1x_2\).

\(x_1^2\): \(4 + 4 + 1 + 1 = 10\). \(x_2^2\): \(1 + 1 + 9 + 9 = 20\). \(x_1x_2\): \(2 + 2 + 3 + 3 = 10\).

Now divide by \(n\): give the two variances and the covariance.

\(10/4 = 2.5\), \(20/4 = 5\), \(10/4 = 2.5\).

3. Projecting onto a direction

In plain words

Shine a light so each point casts a shadow on a line through the origin. The projection of a point is where its shadow lands, measured along the line. For a direction given by a unit vector \(\varphi\) (length 1), the shadow’s position is just the dot product \(\varphi^Tx\) (Toolkit 1). PCA asks: along which direction are the shadows most spread out?

\[z_i = \varphi^Tx_i = \varphi_1x_{i1} + \varphi_2x_{i2},\qquad \|\varphi\| = 1\]

For centred data the projections have mean 0, and their variance is

\[\boxed{\frac1n\sum_i(\varphi^Tx_i)^2 = \varphi^T\Sigma\varphi = \Sigma_{11}\varphi_1^2 + 2\Sigma_{12}\varphi_1\varphi_2 + \Sigma_{22}\varphi_2^2}\]

Worked example 2: two ways to the same number. Centred data from example 1, direction \(\varphi = (0.6, 0.8)\) (length \(\sqrt{0.36 + 0.64} = 1\) ✓).

Way 1, project then average the squares:

point \(z = 0.6x_1 + 0.8x_2\) \(z^2\)
(3, 1) 1.8 + 0.8 = 2.6 6.76
(1, 3) 0.6 + 2.4 = 3.0 9
(−1, −3) −0.6 − 2.4 = −3.0 9
(−3, −1) −1.8 − 0.8 = −2.6 6.76
total 0 31.52

Variance \(= 31.52/4 = 7.88\).

Way 2, use \(\Sigma\): \(5(0.36) + 2(3)(0.6)(0.8) + 5(0.64) = 1.8 + 2.88 + 3.2 = 7.88\) ✓.

Worked example 3: comparing directions. With \(\Sigma = \begin{pmatrix}5 & 3\\3 & 5\end{pmatrix}\):

direction \(\varphi\) \(\varphi^T\Sigma\varphi\)
\((1, 0)\) \(5\)
\((0.6, 0.8)\) \(7.88\)
\(\frac1{\sqrt2}(1, 1)\) \(\frac12(5 + 3 + 3 + 5) = 8\)
\(\frac1{\sqrt2}(1, -1)\) \(\frac12(5 - 3 - 3 + 5) = 2\)

The diagonal \((1, 1)/\sqrt2\) wins: the points lie roughly along it.

Common mistakes

  • Using a non-unit direction. \((1, 1)\) has length \(\sqrt2\); using it unnormalised doubles the variance. Divide by the length first.
  • Missing the factor 2 on the cross term \(2\Sigma_{12}\varphi_1\varphi_2\).
  • Projecting uncentred data and calling the mean square a variance.

\(\Sigma = \begin{pmatrix}2 & 1\\1 & 3\end{pmatrix}\) and \(\varphi = (0.6, 0.8)\). Compute \(\varphi^T\Sigma\varphi\).

\(2(0.36) + 2(1)(0.48) + 3(0.64) = 0.72 + 0.96 + 1.92 = 3.6\).

4. Best directions: eigenvectors and eigenvalues

In plain words

The direction with the most spread is the first principal component. The amount of spread along it is the largest eigenvalue \(\lambda_1\) of \(\Sigma\). The next best direction, at right angles to the first, gives \(\lambda_2\), and so on. The eigenvalues add up to the total variance (the sum of the diagonal), so \(\lambda_1/\sum\lambda\) is the fraction of the spread the first component captures: the proportion of variance explained (PVE).

For a \(2\times2\) matrix \(\begin{pmatrix}a & b\\b & c\end{pmatrix}\):

\[\lambda = \frac{a + c}{2} \pm \sqrt{\Big(\frac{a - c}{2}\Big)^2 + b^2}\]

With equal diagonal (\(a = c\)) the eigenvalues are \(a \pm b\) and the directions are the diagonals \(\frac1{\sqrt2}(1, 1)\) and \(\frac1{\sqrt2}(1, -1)\).

Worked example 4. \(\Sigma = \begin{pmatrix}5 & 3\\3 & 5\end{pmatrix}\): \(\lambda = 5 \pm 3\), so \(\lambda_1 = 8\), \(\lambda_2 = 2\) (matching example 3). Total \(10 = 5 + 5\) ✓. PVE of PC1 \(= 8/10 = 0.8\).

\(\Sigma = \begin{pmatrix}2 & 1\\1 & 3\end{pmatrix}\): \(\frac{5}{2} \pm \sqrt{0.25 + 1} = 2.5 \pm 1.118\), so \(\lambda_1 = 3.618\), \(\lambda_2 = 1.382\). Total \(5\) ✓. PVE \(= 3.618/5 = 0.724\). (Compare: the direction \((0.6, 0.8)\) gave 3.6, just below the best 3.618.)

\(\Sigma = \begin{pmatrix}5 & 2\\2 & 5\end{pmatrix}\). Give both eigenvalues and the PVE of the first component.

Equal diagonal: \(5 \pm 2\), so \(7\) and \(3\). Total \(10\). PVE \(= 0.7\).

Stage test

(2023 Q1(c)i.) Compute \((1, 2, -3)\cdot(5, -4, -1)\).

\(5 - 8 + 3 = 0\), so the vectors are orthogonal.

Centred data \((3, 1), (1, 3), (-1, -3), (-3, -1)\). What is the variance (with \(\frac1n\)) of the projections onto \(\frac1{\sqrt2}(1, 1)\)?

Projections \(\frac{4}{\sqrt2}, \frac4{\sqrt2}, -\frac4{\sqrt2}, -\frac4{\sqrt2}\), each squared \(8\); mean \(8\).

Why must \(\varphi\) be a unit vector when maximising \(\varphi^T\Sigma\varphi\)?

Doubling \(\varphi\) multiplies \(\varphi^T\Sigma\varphi\) by 4. Fixing the length makes “direction” the only choice.
