Bayes classifier and KNN

Your first two models, built entirely from Part A. The Bayes classifier is Bayes' rule plus argmax: the best possible rule, if only you knew the true probabilities. KNN is distances plus a vote: a practical way to estimate those probabilities from data. Swap the vote for an average and you have KNN regression.

1. The Bayes classifier, assembled

In plain words

If an oracle told you \(P(Y = k\mid X = x)\) for every \(x\), the best you could do is always bet on the most likely class. You’d still be wrong sometimes (when a less likely class happens), and that unavoidable error is the Bayes error rate: the floor for every classifier.

\[\hat Y(x) = \underbrace{\arg\max_k}_{\text{A2}}\ \underbrace{P(Y = k\mid X = x)}_{\text{A6}},\qquad \text{Bayes error} = 1 - \mathbb E_X\Big[\max_kP(Y = k\mid X)\Big]\]

Worked example 1: a Bayes error by hand. \(X\) takes the values 1, 2, 3, 4, each with probability \(\frac14\). The true \(P(Y = 1\mid x)\):

\(x\) \(P(Y = 1\mid x)\) \(P(Y = 0\mid x)\) Bayes predicts error at \(x\) = \(1 - \max\)
1 0.1 0.9 0 0.1
2 0.4 0.6 0 0.4
3 0.7 0.3 1 0.3
4 0.9 0.1 1 0.1
average 0.225

Bayes error \(= \frac14(0.1 + 0.4 + 0.3 + 0.1) = 0.225\). No classifier, however clever, can get below 22.5% error on this problem. The Bayes decision boundary is between \(x = 2\) and \(x = 3\), where \(P(Y = 1\mid x)\) crosses 0.5.

Common mistakes

  • Bayes error = 0 for a “perfect” classifier. Only if the classes never overlap. Overlap makes some error unavoidable.
  • Averaging \(\min\) instead of \(1 - \max\). With two classes they’re the same; with three or more, \(1 - \max\) is right.
  • Forgetting to weight by how often each \(x\) occurs (here all \(\frac14\)).

\(X\) is 1 or 2 with equal probability. Three classes: at \(x = 1\) the true probabilities are \((0.8, 0.1, 0.1)\); at \(x = 2\) they are \((0.3, 0.7, 0)\). Give the Bayes error at each \(x\) and overall.

\(x = 1\): \(1 - 0.8 = 0.2\). \(x = 2\): \(1 - 0.7 = 0.3\). Overall \(\frac12(0.2 + 0.3) = 0.25\).

2. KNN, assembled

In plain words

We never know the true posterior. KNN estimates it locally: look at the \(K\) training points nearest to \(x_0\) and count. For a number instead of a class, average their responses.

\[\tilde P(Y = k\mid x_0) = \underbrace{\frac1K\sum_{i\in\mathcal N_0}\mathbb 1(y_i = k)}_{\text{A2: a mean of indicators}},\qquad \hat f(x_0) = \frac1K\sum_{i\in\mathcal N_0}y_i,\qquad \mathcal N_0 = K\text{ nearest by }\underbrace{\|x_i - x_0\|_2}_{\text{A1}}\]

Worked example 2: KNN regression with three values of \(K\). \(x = (1, 2, 4, 7, 8)\), \(y = (3, 5, 4, 10, 12)\). Predict at \(x_0 = 6\).

\(x_i\) \(y_i\) \(\lvert x_i - 6\rvert\) rank
1 3 5 5
2 5 4 4
4 4 2 2 (tie)
7 10 1 1
8 12 2 2 (tie)
\(K\) neighbours’ \(y\) \(\hat f(6)\)
1 10 10
3 10, 4, 12 26/3 = 8.667
5 all 34/5 = 6.8

The tie at ranks 2 and 3 is harmless for \(K = 3\) (both are in). It would matter for \(K = 2\). As \(K\) grows the prediction drifts towards the overall mean \(\bar y = 6.8\): smoother, less flexible.

Common mistakes

  • Including \(x_0\) itself when predicting at a training point and calling that the test error. With \(K = 1\) that gives 0 error every time: it’s the training error.
  • Forgetting to rescale inputs with different units (Toolkit 1).
  • Ties at the \(K\)-th place (Toolkit 2): check before you average.

Try it with me. Same data, now at \(x_0 = 3\).

Give the five distances \(\lvert x_i - 3\rvert\).

\(2, 1, 1, 4, 5\).

Give the KNN regression prediction with \(K = 2\) and with \(K = 3\).

Nearest two: \(x = 2, 4\) (\(y = 5, 4\)), so \(4.5\). Add the next, \(x = 1\) (\(y = 3\)): \((5 + 4 + 3)/3 = 4\).

3. The knob: \(K\)

In plain words

\(K = 1\) follows every training point, noise included: zero training error, high variance. Large \(K\) averages over many points: steady but blurry, so high bias. \(K = n\) predicts the same thing everywhere. Choose \(K\) by validation error, which is typically U-shaped in \(1/K\).

\(K\) flexibility bias variance training error
1 highest low high 0
moderate medium medium medium small
\(n\) lowest (constant) high low large

As \(K\) increases in KNN, which happens?

The Bayes error is a property of the problem, not of the classifier.

Stage test

KNN regression with \(K = 3\): the three nearest neighbours of \(x_0\) have \(y = 2, 6, 7\). What is the prediction?

\((2 + 6 + 7)/3 = 5\).

(4 marks; 2023 Q2.) Explain how KNN classifies a new point, and how it can be changed to do regression. How does \(K\) affect flexibility?

KNN computes the distance (usually Euclidean, \(\sqrt{\sum_j(x_j - x_{0j})^2}\)) from \(x_0\) to every training point and takes the \(K\) closest. For classification it estimates \(P(Y = k\mid x_0)\) by the fraction of those neighbours with label \(k\) and predicts the most common class, imitating the Bayes classifier. For regression it predicts the mean of the neighbours’ \(y\) values. With \(K = 1\) the fit follows every training point (training error 0, high variance); larger \(K\) averages over more points, giving a smoother, less flexible fit with more bias and less variance. \(K\) is chosen by validation error.
