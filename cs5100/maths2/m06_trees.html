Regression and classification trees

A tree chops the input space into boxes with yes/no questions and predicts the same thing everywhere in a box: the mean (regression) or the majority class (classification). Each split is chosen greedily to make the boxes as pure as possible, scored by RSS or impurity. Then it's pruned with "fit + α × leaves". Every one of those is a Part A tool; this stage grows and prunes a tree by hand.

1. Regression trees: choose the split with the smallest RSS

In plain words

Try every possible cut point. For each, split the points into left and right, predict each side by its own mean, and add up the squared errors on both sides. Keep the cut with the smallest total. Then repeat inside each box. It’s greedy: each split is the best one now, without looking ahead.

\[\text{RSS}(\text{cut }s) = \sum_{i:\,x_i < s}(y_i - \bar y_L)^2 + \sum_{i:\,x_i \ge s}(y_i - \bar y_R)^2\]

Candidate cuts are the midpoints between consecutive sorted \(x\) values.

Worked example 1. \(x = (1, 2, 3, 4, 5)\), \(y = (2, 3, 9, 10, 11)\).

cut left \(y\) \(\bar y_L\) RSS left right \(y\) \(\bar y_R\) RSS right total
1.5 2 2 0 3, 9, 10, 11 8.25 38.75 38.75
2.5 2, 3 2.5 0.5 9, 10, 11 10 2 2.5
3.5 2, 3, 9 4.667 28.667 10, 11 10.5 0.5 29.167
4.5 2, 3, 9, 10 6 50 11 11 0 50

Best cut: \(x < 2.5\). The tree predicts 2.5 for \(x < 2.5\) and 10 for \(x \ge 2.5\).

How one RSS cell is computed (cut 2.5, right): gaps to 10 are \(-1, 0, 1\); squares \(1 + 0 + 1 = 2\).

Common mistakes

  • Predicting with the overall mean on both sides. Each side uses its own mean.
  • Squaring raw \(y\) values. RSS is about gaps to each leaf’s mean (the same slip as the K-means SSE).
  • Cut points at data values. Use midpoints, so it’s clear which side each point falls on.
  • Only checking one side. The total is left RSS + right RSS.

Try it with me. \(x = (1, 2, 3, 4)\), \(y = (4, 6, 8, 20)\).

For the cut at 3.5, give the left mean, the left RSS and the right RSS.

Left \(\{4, 6, 8\}\): mean 6, gaps \(-2, 0, 2\), RSS 8. Right \(\{20\}\): RSS 0.

Now give the total RSS for all three cuts. Which is best?

Cut 1.5: left \(\{4\}\) RSS 0; right \(\{6, 8, 20\}\) mean 11.333, gaps \(-5.333, -3.333, 8.667\), RSS \(28.44 + 11.11 + 75.11 = 114.67\). Cut 2.5: left \(\{4, 6\}\) RSS 2; right \(\{8, 20\}\) mean 14, RSS \(36 + 36 = 72\); total 74. Cut 3.5: total 8. Best: 3.5, which isolates the outlying 20.

2. Classification trees: choose the split with the smallest impurity

Same search, scored by the size-weighted Gini or entropy of the children (Toolkit 10). Leaves predict the majority class.

Worked example 2. \(x = (1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6)\), labels A, A, B, A, B, B. Gini by cut:

cut left right Gini L Gini R weighted
1.5 A A B A B B 0 0.48 \(\frac56(0.48) = 0.4\)
2.5 A A B A B B 0 0.375 $(0.375) = $ 0.25
3.5 A A B A B B 0.444 0.444 0.444
4.5 A A B A B B 0.375 0 $(0.375) = $ 0.25
5.5 A A B A B B 0.48 0 0.4

Two cuts tie at 0.25 (parent Gini 0.5, so gain 0.25 either way). Either is a correct answer; say that they tie. Note the “middle” cut 3.5 is worst.

3. Pruning: fit + α × leaves

Grow a big tree, then for each \(\alpha\) keep the subtree minimising \(\text{RSS}(T) + \alpha\lvert T\rvert\) (Toolkit 5). Choose \(\alpha\) by validation or cross-validation. Larger \(\alpha\) = fewer leaves = less flexible.

Worked example 3. Continue example 1. The 1-leaf tree predicts \(\bar y = 7\) everywhere: RSS \(= 25 + 16 + 4 + 9 + 16 = 70\). The 2-leaf tree has RSS 2.5. Keep the split when \(2.5 + 2\alpha < 70 + \alpha\), i.e. when \(\alpha < 67.5\). The split is worth it unless each leaf “costs” more than 67.5.
