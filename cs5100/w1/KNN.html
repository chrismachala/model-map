K-nearest neighbours (KNN)

"To guess for someone new, look at the K most similar people you already know and go with the majority." That's the whole model.

Question type: classification here. The same idea does regression in week 2 (KNN regression) and comes back as a classifier in week 3.
Weeks: 1, 2 and 3.
Book: ISLP 2.2.3 (second half, “K-Nearest Neighbors”). Also 4.7 lab mentions it.
Techniques on this page: distance, neighbourhood vote, choosing \(K\), normalising features.

The problem it solves

The Bayes classifier is perfect, but it needs the true chance of each category, which we never know. KNN is the simplest possible way to estimate those chances from data: the chance of “default” for this person is roughly the fraction of similar people who defaulted.

It assumes no shape for the rule (it’s non-parametric), so it can follow any pattern, however curvy. That flexibility is also its weakness: it needs a lot of data, and you have to choose how many neighbours to listen to.

The idea in everyday words

You move to a new street and want to guess whether your neighbours recycle. You look at the 5 nearest houses: 4 have recycling bins out. Your guess: probably yes, with a “chance” of 4/5.

Words you’ll hear

Word Plain meaning
\(K\) how many neighbours you look at. You choose it.
neighbours, \(\mathcal{N}_{x_0}\) the \(K\) training points closest to the new point \(x_0\). The curly N is just a name for that group.
Euclidean distance ordinary straight-line distance, like with a ruler
non-parametric doesn’t assume any shape for the rule; just uses the data directly
flexible able to bend to follow the data closely
overfitting bending so much it follows the luck, not the pattern

Technique 1: measuring distance

Problem it solves: “closest” needs a definition when there are several inputs.

For two points, square the difference in each input, add them up, and take the square root (Pythagoras):

\[\text{distance}(x, x_0) = \sqrt{(x_1 - x_{01})^2 + (x_2 - x_{02})^2 + \dots + (x_p - x_{0p})^2}\]

With one input it’s just \(|x - x_0|\) (the gap, ignoring sign). The week 3 lab skips the square root because it doesn’t change which points are closest.

From \((1, 1)\) to \((0, 0)\): \(\sqrt{1^2 + 1^2} = \sqrt2 \approx 1.41\). From \((1,1)\) to \((3, 0)\): \(\sqrt{2^2 + 1^2} = \sqrt5 \approx 2.24\).

Technique 2: the neighbourhood vote

Problem it solves: turning the neighbours into a chance for each category, and then a decision.

  1. Find the \(K\) training points closest to the new point \(x_0\).
  2. The estimated chance of category \(k\) is the fraction of those neighbours in category \(k\): \[\tilde P(Y = k \mid X = x_0) = \frac{1}{K}\sum_{\text{neighbours}} \mathbb{1}(y = k)\] The sum with the indicator \(\mathbb 1\) just counts neighbours in category \(k\). Dividing by \(K\) turns the count into a fraction.
  3. Pick the category with the biggest fraction (the Bayes classifier rule, using these estimates).

Slide typo: slide equation (33) leaves out the \(\frac1K\). Without it you get a count, not a chance. The book (eq. 2.12) and the slide’s own worked example (\(\tfrac13\), \(\tfrac23\)) include it.

Worked example (one input).

\(x\) 1 2 3 4 5 6
\(y\) A A B B B A

New point \(x_0 = 3.4\). Gaps: \(x{=}3\): 0.4, \(x{=}4\): 0.6, \(x{=}2\): 1.4, \(x{=}5\): 1.6, \(x{=}1\): 2.4, \(x{=}6\): 2.6.

  • \(K = 1\): nearest is \(x = 3\) (B). Predict B.
  • \(K = 3\): B, B, A. Chance of B is \(2/3\). Predict B.
  • \(K = 5\): B, B, A, B, A. Chance of B is \(3/5\). Predict B.
  • \(K = 6\): everyone, 3 A and 3 B: a tie. That’s why odd \(K\) is preferred with two categories.

Technique 3: choosing \(K\) (the bendiness dial)

Problem it solves: KNN has no numbers to fit, but it has one setting, \(K\), and it decides everything.

  • Small \(K\) (like 1): very bendy. The boundary between categories is jagged and chases individual points. Training error is zero for \(K = 1\) (each training point’s nearest neighbour is itself), but that’s memorising, not learning. Low bias, high variance.
  • Large \(K\) (like 100): very smooth, nearly a straight boundary. It ignores real local patterns. High bias, low variance.
  • In between (the slides show \(K = 10\)): can get very close to the Bayes classifier.

If you plot error against \(1/K\) (so more bendy is to the right), training error keeps falling, but test error falls and then rises again: the U shape from the big picture. The best \(K\) is at the bottom of the U. You choose it with a validation set (week 2), never with training error.

Technique 4: normalising features (put inputs on the same scale)

Problem it solves: distance adds up raw differences, so an input measured in big units drowns out the others.

House age ranges 0 to 100 years; number of rooms ranges 3 to 9. Without fixing this, “nearest” is almost entirely about age. The fix is to rescale each input to have average 0 and spread 1 (subtract the column’s mean, divide by its standard deviation). The week 2 lab’s normalize() does exactly this.

Strengths and weaknesses

Good at Bad at
any shape of boundary, however curvy needs lots of data
no assumptions slow to predict (measures distance to every training point)
very easy to explain many inputs make “nearby” meaningless (the curse of dimensionality)
tells you nothing about why (no coefficients to read)
