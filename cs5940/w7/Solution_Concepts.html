Solution concepts

Given a payoff matrix, what will rational agents do, and what would be good for them? Four tests answer that: dominant strategies, Nash equilibrium, Pareto optimality and social welfare. They often disagree, and that disagreement is the whole point of the prisoner's dilemma.

Kind: multi-agent interaction.
Week: 7, Part III (solution concepts).
Book: Wooldridge 11.3 Solution concepts (p. 229): 11.3.1 Dominant strategies (p. 230), 11.3.2 Nash equilibria (pp. 230 to 233), 11.3.3 Pareto efficiency (p. 233), 11.3.4 Maximising social welfare (p. 235); 11.4 Competitive and zero-sum interactions (pp. 235 to 236).
Techniques on this page: best responses, dominant strategies, pure Nash equilibrium, mixed strategies and matching pennies, Pareto optimality, social welfare, zero-sum games.

The problem it solves

“How will a rational agent behave?” has no single answer, so game theory offers solution concepts: precise tests an outcome or strategy can pass or fail. Two describe what self-interested agents will do (dominant strategy, Nash equilibrium). Two describe what’s good for the group (Pareto optimality, social welfare). A quiz will hand you a 2 × 2 matrix and ask which outcomes pass which test.

Throughout this page payoffs are written (i, j) with \(i\) choosing the row and \(j\) the column.

Words you’ll hear

Word Plain meaning
best response the action that does best for you, given what the other player does
dominant strategy does at least as well as anything else whatever the other player does
Nash equilibrium (NE) a pair of strategies where nobody gains by changing on their own
unilateral deviation one player switching while the other stays put
Pareto optimal (efficient) no other outcome makes someone better off without making someone worse off
Pareto dominated some other outcome is at least as good for everyone and better for someone
social welfare the sum of everyone’s utilities: “the total money in the system”
zero-sum utilities always add to zero: my gain is your loss
strictly competitive preferences diametrically opposed

Technique 1: best responses (the tool for everything else)

Problem it solves: a mechanical way to read any 2 × 2 game.

For each column (each choice of \(j\)), find \(i\)’s best row and mark it. For each row (each choice of \(i\)), find \(j\)’s best column and mark it. Then:

  • a player whose best response is the same whatever the other does has a dominant strategy;
  • a cell where both payoffs are marked is a pure Nash equilibrium.

Technique 2: dominant strategies

Problem it solves: the easiest solution: one choice is never worse.

Strategy \(s_i\) is dominant for \(i\) if, whatever \(j\) chooses, \(i\) does at least as well with \(s_i\) as with any other strategy.

The slides’ example, (i, j):

\(j\): \(s_3\) \(j\): \(s_4\)
\(i\): \(s_1\) (1, −1) (3, −3)
\(i\): \(s_2\) (−2, 2) (0, 0)

Against \(s_3\): \(s_1\) gives 1 > −2. Against \(s_4\): \(s_1\) gives 3 > 0. So \(s_1\) dominates \(s_2\) for \(i\). (And \(j\): against \(s_1\), \(s_3\) gives −1 > −3; against \(s_2\), 2 > 0, so \(s_3\) is dominant for \(j\). Every cell adds to 0: it’s zero-sum too.)

Not every game has a dominant strategy (matching pennies doesn’t). If both players have one, the outcome where both play it is the obvious prediction and is always a Nash equilibrium.

Technique 3: pure-strategy Nash equilibrium

Problem it solves: a stable prediction even when nobody has a dominant strategy.

\((s_1, s_2)\) is a Nash equilibrium if (1) given \(i\) plays \(s_1\), \(j\) can do no better than \(s_2\), and (2) given \(j\) plays \(s_2\), \(i\) can do no better than \(s_1\). No incentive to deviate unilaterally.

The slides’ example, (i, j):

\(j\): c \(j\): d
\(i\): a (5, 5) (8, 2)
\(i\): b (2, 8) (3, 3)

Check each cell:

  • (a, c): \(i\) switching to b gets 2 < 5; \(j\) switching to d gets 2 < 5. Stable: NE.
  • (a, d): \(j\) would switch to c (5 > 2). Not NE.
  • (b, c): \(i\) would switch to a (5 > 2). Not NE.
  • (b, d): \(i\) would switch to a (8 > 3). Not NE.

So (a, c) is the unique NE. (Here a and c also happen to be dominant for \(i\) and \(j\): a beats b in both columns, c beats d in both rows. When both players have a dominant strategy, playing them is always a Nash equilibrium, but a game can have a NE with no dominant strategies at all, like chicken.)

Some games have no pure NE (matching pennies); some have several (game of chicken: both \((C, D)\) and \((D, C)\)).

Technique 4: mixed strategies and matching pennies

Problem it solves: gives an equilibrium when no pure one exists.

Matching pennies: both choose heads or tails; same face → \(i\) wins (+1, −1); different → \(j\) wins (−1, +1).

\(j\): H \(j\): T
\(i\): H (1, −1) (−1, 1)
\(i\): T (−1, 1) (1, −1)

No pure pair is stable: whatever is chosen, somebody wishes they’d done otherwise. Allow mixed strategies: each plays heads with probability 0.5. Then \(EU_i(H) = EU_i(T) = EU_j(H) = EU_j(T) = 0\), so neither can gain by changing: a mixed-strategy NE.

The indifference trick (not spelled out on the slides, but it’s how 0.5 is found): \(j\) mixes so that \(i\) is indifferent. If \(j\) plays H with probability \(q\): \(EU_i(H) = 2q - 1\), \(EU_i(T) = 1 - 2q\). Equal when \(q = 0.5\).

Nash’s theorem (Wooldridge p. 233): every game with finitely many strategies has at least one Nash equilibrium in mixed strategies.

Technique 5: Pareto optimality

Problem it solves: spots outcomes that waste value.

An outcome is Pareto optimal if no other outcome makes one agent better off without making another worse off. Plot each outcome as a point (i’s payoff, j’s payoff): the Pareto optimal ones are those with nothing up and to the right of them.

  • If an outcome is Pareto optimal, at least one agent would be reluctant to move away.
  • If it isn’t, there’s another outcome everyone likes at least as much, so “reasonable” agents should agree to move.

Prisoner’s dilemma (i, j): (D, D) = (2, 2), (D, C) = (4, 1), (C, D) = (1, 4), (C, C) = (3, 3). (C, C) beats (D, D) for both, so (D, D) is not Pareto optimal. (4, 1), (1, 4) and (3, 3): none is beaten on both coordinates, so all three are Pareto optimal.

Technique 6: social welfare

Problem it solves: the best outcome for the system as a whole.

\[sw(\omega) = \sum_{i \in Ag} u_i(\omega)\]

“The total amount of money in the system.” Appropriate when the whole system has a single owner, so individual shares don’t matter. In the prisoner’s dilemma: (D, D) = 4, (D, C) = 5, (C, D) = 5, (C, C) = 6 is the maximum.

A social-welfare maximising outcome is always Pareto optimal (if something were better for someone and no worse for anyone, the total would be higher). The converse fails: (4, 1) is Pareto optimal but has welfare 5 < 6.

Technique 7: zero-sum and strictly competitive games

Problem it solves: recognises the worst case for cooperation.

Preferences diametrically opposed = strictly competitive. Zero-sum: \(u_i(\omega) + u_j(\omega) = 0\) for all \(\omega\). For me to gain you must lose; my best outcome is your worst. Matching pennies, chess (win/lose) and the dominant-strategy example above are zero-sum. Real zero-sum encounters are rare, but people often act as if they were in one.

In a zero-sum game every outcome is Pareto optimal (any gain for one is a loss for the other) and social welfare is always 0, so those two concepts tell you nothing there.

Summary table to memorise. Prisoner’s dilemma: dominant strategy D for both; Nash equilibrium (D, D) only; Pareto optimal every outcome except (D, D); social welfare maximised at (C, C). The only Nash equilibrium is the only outcome that isn’t Pareto optimal.
