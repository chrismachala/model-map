Utilities, preferences and games

With other agents around, what's best for you depends on what they do. Game theory gives the vocabulary: who the players are, what each can do, and a utility number for every combination. This page is the notation; the next one is how to solve a game.

Kind: multi-agent interaction.
Week: 7, Parts I to III (what MAS are; utilities and preferences; game theory foundations).
Book: Wooldridge ch. 11: 11.1 Utilities and preferences (pp. 223 to 226), 11.2 Setting the scene (pp. 226 to 229). PDF page = book page + 21.
Techniques on this page: strategic interaction, utility functions and preference orderings, utility vs money, the game tuple, pure and mixed strategies, reading payoff matrices, the rational choice when one action is always better.

The problem it solves

Weeks 1 to 6 were about one agent in an environment. In a multi-agent system several autonomous agents share an environment, collaborate or compete, and their decisions are interdependent. They can have different goals, different internal models, different spheres of influence, and be linked by relationships.

In a single-agent world the agent just maximises its utility. In a multi-agent world interaction becomes strategic: my best action depends on yours, and yours on mine. Week 5’s hide-and-seek already showed it: when the others learn, your environment keeps changing.

The question for the week: how should agents act in the presence of other agents?

Technique 1: strategic interaction

Problem it solves: says what changes when there are other agents.

A strategy is a complete plan of action describing what the agent will do, conditioned on the observed state of the environment. It can be a single unconditioned action (“I will always do \(x\)”), or random (“I chose \(x\) on a gut feeling”).

Many applications are naturally multi-agent: markets and auctions, traffic and self-driving cars, negotiation, games, robots sharing a warehouse, LLM agents dealing with other LLM agents.

Technique 2: utility functions and preference orderings

Problem it solves: turns “what the agent wants” into numbers you can compare.

Each agent has \(u_i : \Omega \to \mathbb{R}\). Utilities give preference orderings: \(\omega \succeq_i \omega'\) means \(u_i(\omega) \ge u_i(\omega')\); \(\omega \succ_i \omega'\) means \(u_i(\omega) > u_i(\omega')\).

Utility is not money (but it’s a useful analogy). The typical relationship is concave: the first £1,000 matters more than going from £1,000,000 to £1,001,000 (Wooldridge Figure 11.2).

Utility-based vs goal-based agents. A goal is all-or-nothing; utility says how desirable each resulting state is, so the agent can trade off. The slides’ technical examples: does a racing agent collect rewards or finish the race? Collect points or avoid losing? The answer depends entirely on how the designer wrote the utility, which is week 4’s reward hacking again.

Ethics: as agents become able to cause harm (self-driving cars), who decides their values, and how do we align them with intended behaviour and with society?

Technique 3: the game

Problem it solves: a compact model of any one-shot interaction.

\[G = (N, \{A_i\}_{i \in N}, \{U_i\}_{i \in N})\]

  • Players \(N = \{1, \dots, n\}\): the decision-makers.
  • Actions \(A_i\): player \(i\)’s immediate choices.
  • Action profile \(\omega = (a_1, \dots, a_n)\): one action each, the outcome.
  • Utility functions \(U_i : A_1 \times \dots \times A_n \to \mathbb{R}\): each player’s preferences over outcomes (ordinal or cardinal).

With two players and two actions each (C = cooperate, D = defect) there are four outcomes: \((D, D), (D, C), (C, D), (C, C)\), written with \(i\)’s action first.

Technique 4: pure and mixed strategies

Problem it solves: some games have no sensible deterministic choice.

  • A pure strategy picks one action \(a_i \in A_i\) for sure.
  • A mixed strategy is a probability distribution \(\sigma_i \in \Delta(A_i)\) over the pure strategies, with \(\sum_{a_i} \sigma_i(a_i) = 1\).

Expected utility of a mixed strategy. In matching pennies, \(i\) wins (+1) when the faces match and loses (−1) otherwise. If \(j\) plays heads with probability \(q\), then \(EU_i(\text{heads}) = q(1) + (1 - q)(-1) = 2q - 1\) and \(EU_i(\text{tails}) = q(-1) + (1-q)(1) = 1 - 2q\). At \(q = 0.5\) both are 0: \(i\) is indifferent. That’s why 50/50 is the equilibrium (next page).

Technique 5: reading a payoff matrix

Problem it solves: four outcomes and eight numbers at a glance.

Each cell is one outcome and holds both players’ utilities. The slides (following Wooldridge) put \(i\) as the column player and \(j\) as the row player, with \(i\)’s payoff in the top-right of each cell and \(j\)’s in the bottom-left. Many equivalent layouts exist (the slides show several), so in a quiz always check whose number is whose before answering.

The slides switch layouts. In the prisoner’s dilemma \(i\) is the column player; in the dominant-strategy and Nash examples \(i\) is the row player. This page and the next always write payoffs as (i’s utility, j’s utility) with \(i\) on the rows, which is the most common textbook form.

Technique 6: the rational choice when one action always wins

Problem it solves: the easiest kind of game to solve.

The slides’ example. \(u_i(D, D) = 1\), \(u_i(D, C) = 1\), \(u_i(C, D) = 4\), \(u_i(C, C) = 4\) and \(u_j(D,D) = 1\), \(u_j(D, C) = 4\), \(u_j(C, D) = 1\), \(u_j(C, C) = 4\).

\(i\)’s preferences: \((C, C) \succeq_i (C, D) \succ_i (D, C) \succeq_i (D, D)\). Every outcome that follows from C is better for \(i\) than every outcome that follows from D, whatever \(j\) does. So C is the rational choice for \(i\). By the same argument (\(u_j = 4\) whenever \(j\) plays C) C is rational for \(j\) too.

As a matrix, (i, j), \(i\) on rows:

\(j\): D \(j\): C
\(i\): D (1, 1) (1, 4)
\(i\): C (4, 1) (4, 4)

The quiz-style trap: “\(i\) prefers C” is about \(i\)’s numbers only. Don’t look at \(j\)’s column when deciding for \(i\).

Do humans and LLMs play “rationally”? The lecture’s data: humans cooperated about 64% of the time in a social-dilemma game; LLM agents (Haiku, GPT-4.1 nano, Grok) ranged from about 8% to 100% depending on the prompt configuration. Rational-choice theory is a model, not a description.
