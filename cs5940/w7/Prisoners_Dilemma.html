The prisoner's dilemma

Two agents each do what's individually rational and both end up worse off than if they had cooperated. That's the fundamental problem of multi-agent interaction. This page covers the dilemma itself, the ways people have tried to recover cooperation (programs, repetition, Axelrod's tournament), the other 2 × 2 games, and the beauty contest.

Kind: multi-agent interaction.
Week: 7, Parts IV to VI.
Book: Wooldridge 11.5 The prisoner’s dilemma (pp. 236 to 240), 11.5.1 The shadow of the future (pp. 240 to 243), 11.5.2 Program equilibria (pp. 243 to 245), 11.6 Other symmetric 2 × 2 interactions (pp. 245 to 248, Table 11.1 on p. 246), 11.7 Representing multiagent scenarios (pp. 248 to 249). Skip 11.8 dependence relations.
Techniques on this page: the dilemma and its payoffs, the four solution concepts applied, recovering cooperation, program equilibria, the iterated game and backwards induction, Axelrod’s strategies, the other 2 × 2 orderings, beauty contests.

The problem it solves

The prisoner’s dilemma shows that individually rational choices can produce a collectively bad outcome, and seems to imply that cooperation won’t happen among self-interested agents. Yet it’s everywhere: nuclear arms reduction (“why don’t I keep mine?”), free riders on public transport, TV licences in the UK. Agent designers need to know when cooperation can be recovered, and how.

Technique 1: the dilemma and its payoffs

Problem it solves: the canonical model of “cooperate or betray”.

Two prisoners, separate cells, no communication. If one defects and the other stays silent, the defector goes free and the other gets 3 years. Both defect: 2 years each. Both stay silent: 1 year each.

Utilities: freed = 4, 1 year = 3, 2 years = 2, 3 years = 1. Any payoffs work as long as T > R > P > S.

Payoffs (i, j), \(i\) on the rows (the slides draw \(i\) as the column player; same numbers):

\(j\): D \(j\): C
\(i\): D (2, 2) punishment (4, 1) temptation / sucker
\(i\): C (1, 4) sucker / temptation (3, 3) reward

Technique 2: the four solution concepts, applied

Problem it solves: the most-asked quiz matrix.

Concept Answer Why
dominant strategy D for both if \(j\) defects, D gives 2 > 1; if \(j\) cooperates, D gives 4 > 3
Nash equilibrium (D, D) only from (D, D) switching to C drops you from 2 to 1; from any other cell somebody gains by switching to D
Pareto optimal all except (D, D) (C, C) is better for both than (D, D)
max social welfare (C, C), total 6 vs 5, 5, 4

“Defection guarantees no worse than 2; cooperation guarantees at most… well, as little as 1.” So both defect and get 2, when they could both have had 3. That gap is the paradox.

Technique 3: recovering cooperation

Problem it solves: is game-theoretic rationality wrong, or is the game set up wrong?

The slides’ list of counter-arguments:

  • We are not all Machiavelli! People care about others, so the payoffs aren’t really T > R > P > S.
  • The other prisoner is my twin! If we think alike, we’ll choose alike, so only (C, C) and (D, D) are really possible, and (C, C) is better. (Wooldridge calls this the fallacy of the twins: if I can make my twin choose just by thinking it, there aren’t really two players, so it isn’t the prisoner’s dilemma at all.)
  • Program equilibria and mediators (technique 4).
  • The shadow of the future (technique 5).

In module terms: real agents are heterogeneous, and the environment is dynamic and uncertain.

Technique 4: program equilibria

Problem it solves: lets you play “I’ll cooperate if he will”.

Each agent submits a program to a mediator, which runs them together. Programs can be conditioned on the other’s program.

IF TheirProgram == ThisProgram THEN
    DO(C)
ELSE
    DO(D)
END-IF

(== is textual comparison.) The best response to this program is to submit the same program, giving (C, C). And you can’t get the sucker’s payoff by submitting it: against anything different it defects.

Technique 5: the iterated game and backwards induction

Problem it solves: does meeting again make cooperation rational?

Play the game repeatedly. If you’ll meet again, defecting now invites retaliation later, so the incentive to defect appears to evaporate. In the infinitely repeated prisoner’s dilemma, cooperation can be rational.

Backwards induction spoils the finite case. Suppose you both know you’ll play exactly \(n\) rounds. In the last round there’s no future, so it’s a one-shot game: defect. Knowing that, round \(n - 1\) is effectively the last “real” round: defect there too. And so on back to round 1. With a fixed, finite, pre-determined, commonly known number of rounds, defection is the best strategy.

Why each condition matters. Unknown number of rounds (each round there’s a 90% chance of another) → no last round to unravel from → cooperation can survive. Known 10 rounds → unravels to all-D. Infinite → no last round.

Technique 6: Axelrod’s tournament

Problem it solves: which strategy does best against a whole population of opponents?

Axelrod (1984) ran computer tournaments of IPD programs. The slides’ strategies:

Strategy Rule
ALLD always defect (the “hawk”)
TIT-FOR-TAT round 0: cooperate; round \(u > 0\): do what the opponent did in round \(u - 1\)
GRIM cooperate; if the opponent ever defects, defect for ever
Pavlov win-stay, lose-shift: repeat your move after a good payoff (T or R), switch after a bad one (P or S)

From Wooldridge (pp. 241 to 243): in Axelrod’s 1980 tournament every program played every other (five games of 200 rounds), and the overall winner was TIT-FOR-TAT, the simplest entry. It won on total score across all opponents, not by beating anyone head to head (against ALLD it loses). Axelrod’s four rules: don’t be envious, don’t be the first to defect (being “nice” was the best predictor of success), reciprocate cooperation and defection, and don’t be too clever.

Five rounds, payoffs 4/3/2/1. TFT vs ALLD: TFT plays C, D, D, D, D; ALLD plays D every time. TFT scores 1 + 2 + 2 + 2 + 2 = 9; ALLD scores 4 + 2 + 2 + 2 + 2 = 12. TFT vs TFT: C every round, 15 each. TFT never beats its opponent in a single match, yet wins tournaments because it collects lots of mutual-cooperation payoffs.

Technique 7: other symmetric 2 × 2 games

Problem it solves: the prisoner’s dilemma is one of many possible preference orderings.

PD ordering for \(i\): \((D, C) \succ_i (C, C) \succ_i (D, D) \succ_i (C, D)\). With four outcomes there are \(4! = 24\) orderings (Wooldridge Table 11.1, p. 246). Examples from the slides:

Ordering for \(i\) Name
\((C, C) \succ (C, D) \succ (D, C) \succ (D, D)\) cooperation dominates
\((C, C) \succ (C, D) \succ (D, D) \succ (D, C)\) cooperation dominates
\((D, C) \succ (C, C) \succ (C, D) \succ (D, D)\) game of chicken
\((D, D) \succ (D, C) \succ (C, C) \succ (C, D)\) defection dominates
\((D, D) \succ (D, C) \succ (C, D) \succ (C, C)\) defection dominates

Chicken differs from the PD only in swapping the bottom two: mutual defection (crash) is the worst outcome, worse than being the sucker (swerving). So there’s no dominant strategy and two pure Nash equilibria, (C, D) and (D, C): whoever swerves loses face, but neither wants the crash.

Representing interactions (11.7): rules can be hard-wired into agents at design time, or written so agents can process them at run time, as in Stanford’s General Game Playing competition (game rules in a logic language, read by the agent before play).

Technique 8: guessing games (the beauty contest)

Problem it solves: shows how deep “I think that you think” reasoning goes, and how far people are from equilibrium.

Two-player: each picks \(x_1, x_2 \in [0, 100]\). Target \(t = \frac{2}{3} \cdot \frac{x_1 + x_2}{2}\). Closest wins; ties split. The lower number always wins when they differ, so the unique Nash equilibrium is (0, 0).

\(n\)-player: \(t = \frac{2}{3} \cdot \frac{1}{n}\sum_i x_i\). Reasoning about others gives successively lower guesses; unique NE: everyone picks 0.

Level-k reasoning. Level 0 picks at random (average 50). Level 1 best-responds to that: \(\frac{2}{3} \times 50 = 33.3\). Level 2: \(\frac{2}{3} \times 33.3 = 22.2\). Level 3: 14.8. Infinite levels → 0. The lecture’s data: humans averaged about 29 (roughly level 1 to 2), a learning model (EWA) about 11; LLM players clustered at different levels depending on the model and prompt.
