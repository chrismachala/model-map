Subsumption (Brooks)

Throw away the symbols and the reasoning. Give the robot a stack of simple behaviours, "if this, do that", and let the most basic one that applies win. Intelligence emerges from the robot interacting with its world.

Kind: reactive agent.
Week: 3 (slides 26 to 33).
Book: Wooldridge 5.1 intro and 5.1.1 The subsumption architecture (pp. 85 to 90), 5.1.5 Limitations (p. 92).
Techniques on this page: Brooks’s three theses, behaviours and inhibition, the action function (and its cost), the Mars explorer, the limitations.

The problem it solves

Deductive and BDI agents pay for symbolic reasoning: turning the world into symbols (transduction), representing it, and now deciding when to reconsider. In a fast, messy physical world that can be too slow.

Brooks (1991) took the radical line: don’t reason at all. Wire perceptions straight to actions in prioritised layers. Decisions take constant time, and surprisingly clever group behaviour can emerge from very simple rules, like ants finding food.

Words you’ll hear

Word or symbol Plain meaning
situated, embodied intelligence lives in a body acting in the real world, not in a disembodied prover
emergent arising from interaction, not programmed directly
behaviour \((c, a)\) a rule: if the percept is in condition set \(c\), do action \(a\)
fires the behaviour’s condition matches the current percept
inhibition \(\prec\) \(b_1 \prec b_2\): \(b_1\) beats \(b_2\) (it’s lower in the hierarchy, more basic)
gradient a signal that’s stronger nearer the base, so “up the gradient” means “towards home”
stigmergy cooperating by leaving signs in the environment (ant pheromones, crumbs)

Technique 1: Brooks’s three theses

Problem it solves: a short-answer classic. Intelligence

  1. can be generated without explicit representations of the symbolic-AI kind;
  2. can be generated without explicit abstract reasoning of that kind;
  3. is an emergent property of certain complex systems.

Two ideas drive it: situatedness and embodiment (real intelligence is in the world) and intelligence and emergence (it comes from interaction with the environment; it’s “in the eye of the beholder”).

Technique 2: behaviours and inhibition

Problem it solves: how the agent is built.

A subsumption architecture is a hierarchy of task-accomplishing behaviours, each a simple “if situation, then action” rule. Behaviours compete; lower layers are more primitive (avoid obstacles) and take precedence.

Formally (slide 30, book p. 87):

  • \(see\) maps states to percepts, as in week 1;
  • a behaviour is \((c, a)\) with \(c \subseteq Per\), \(a \in Ac\); it fires in state \(s\) iff \(see(s) \in c\);
  • the agent’s behaviours \(R\) come with an inhibition relation \(\prec\), a strict total order. \(b_1 \prec b_2\) means \(b_1\) inhibits \(b_2\).

Technique 3: the action function

Problem it solves: picks the action, very fast.

function action(p : Per) : Ac
    var fired : ℘(R)
begin
    fired := {(c, a) | (c, a) ∈ R and p ∈ c}
    for each (c, a) ∈ fired do
        if not (∃(c', a') ∈ fired such that (c', a') ≺ (c, a)) then
            return a
    return null
end

In words: find every behaviour that fires, and return the action of the one no other firing behaviour inhibits. If none fire, do nothing.

Cost. As written, at most \(n^2\) comparisons for \(n\) behaviours. Since \(\prec\) is a total order you can do better: keep the behaviours sorted and return the first that fires, at most \(n\) checks. Wired into hardware it’s constant time. Compare theorem proving, which can be undecidable.

Worked example. Highest priority first: \(b_1\): if cliff then stop; \(b_2\): if bump then reverse; \(b_3\): if light then go_to_light; \(b_4\): if true then wander. Percept {bump, light}.

\(b_2\), \(b_3\) and \(b_4\) fire. \(b_2\) is highest among them: reverse. In inhibition notation, \(b_2 \prec b_3\).

Technique 4: the Mars explorer

Problem it solves: shows cooperation emerging with no communication at all (Steels; slide 31, book pp. 87 to 90).

Robots collect rock samples that lie in clusters at unknown places. A beacon at the base creates a gradient. Behaviours, highest priority first:

  1. If you detect an obstacle, change direction.
  2. If carrying samples and at the base, drop them.
  3. If carrying samples and not at the base, drop 2 crumbs and travel up the gradient.
  4. If you detect a sample, pick it up.
  5. If you sense crumbs, pick up 1 crumb and travel down the gradient.
  6. If true, move randomly.

Worked example: which behaviour wins?

Percept Fire Chosen
at a sample, not carrying 4, 6 4 pick up
carrying, at a sample, not at base 3, 4, 6 3 go home
on crumbs, not carrying, no sample 5, 6 5 follow the trail out
carrying, on crumbs, not at base 3, 5, 6 3 go home
obstacle ahead, carrying 1, 3, 6 1 change direction
nothing special 6 6 random walk

Why the crumbs work. A robot carrying a sample home lays a trail. Others who find it follow it away from base, towards the cluster, picking crumbs up as they go. A trail to an exhausted cluster fades; a productive one is refreshed by every robot coming home along it. The robots never talk: the environment carries the information. That’s implicit cooperation (stigmergy), and an early example of the emergence studied in Part III. In the week 3 notebook’s simulation, crumbs raised deliveries from 25.3 to 35.8 samples on average.

Technique 5: the limitations

Problem it solves: the other half of any “evaluate subsumption” answer (slide 32, book 5.1.5).

  • Local information only: decisions use the current percept, so no long-term or non-local view.
  • Why does it work? Behaviour “emerges”, so the link between rules, world and overall behaviour isn’t understood. You lose BDI’s knowledge-level transparency.
  • Hard to debug when it doesn’t work.
  • No design method: trial and error, and very hard beyond a handful of layers (the book says fewer than about ten).
  • Hard to explain to humans: there’s no symbolic state to report.

Discussion (slide 33): “black-box but effective” today. The same critique fits deep learning and LLM agents, which dominate anyway. A fair position: transparency was traded for capability because it paid off, but the price shows in safety-critical settings, which is why hybrid designs put a transparent layer around an opaque one.
